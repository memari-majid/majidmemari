Unit 07 of 12

Pilot, revise, and retest

What happened when another person tried the solution?

Learn

By the end of this unit

  • Record an actual user task without substituting a supplied example for observation.
  • Use a defect to revise the solution, retest it, and qualify the resulting claim.

Observe the work rather than perform it for the user

A user test asks another person to complete a defined task using your actual version. Give the task and ordinary instructions, then observe. If you must explain a missing step, record the assistance. Helping someone recover is appropriate; hiding the help makes the kit appear more usable than the evidence supports.

Confirm that the reviewer can open the files and has the permitted inputs. State what you are observing and record only the information needed for the exercise. A classroom colleague using fictional questions can provide real evidence about that classroom attempt. The case facts remain fictional; the event, actions, difficulty, and timing are observed.

A revision should respond to a consequential finding. Changing a decorative color after a false refund promise does not address the defect. Keep the failed example, revised instruction, and retest result together. A defect is useful learning evidence when you explain its cause, consequence, and correction.

Compare like with like. Report complete effort and quality using the definitions you chose before testing. If a task is incomplete, show that status rather than inserting a favorable time. If one person performs the second attempt faster, practice may explain part of the difference. A bounded improvement claim is stronger than an unsupported causal claim.

Try

Make a claim the pilot record can support

A separate supplied Cedar Desk cohort has 12 invitees, 9 starters, 6 completers, and 4 who complete another task within 14 days after invitation. Every invitee has the same follow-up window. These fictional counts are separate from the discovery interviews and reply-processing cases.

Inspect the people behind the percentages, then commit a completion claim with a denominator and period. Reveal the changed-denominator challenge and decide whether your wording needs revision. Explain whether any person's behavior changed.

Worked explanation and evidence limits

Use this after trying the decision, or as the complete reading version of the activity. These fixed teaching comparisons are separate from your exploration settings.

A changed denominator is a changed question

Fixed denominator challenge; the same six completions and 14-day observation window
QuestionNumeratorDenominatorResult
How many invitees completed a task?6 completers12 invited people6/12 = 50%
How many starters completed a task?6 completers9 people who started6/9 ≈ 66.7%

No additional person completes a task when the denominator changes. The 50% claim describes everyone invited; the 66.7% claim describes only those who started. Both can be correct if labeled. Neither establishes improved behavior, universal usability, paid demand, or a monthly adoption forecast.

Return among initial completers is 4/6 ≈ 66.7%; return among invitees is 4/12 ≈ 33.3%. Here “return” means completing another task within 14 days after invitation. “Most users returned” is ambiguous. “Four of six initial completers completed another task within 14 days after invitation” is inspectable.

Account for everyone: three did not start, three started but did not complete, two completed only one task, and four completed another task. Those non-overlapping groups sum to twelve. The counts alone do not tell you whether access, need, instructions, assistance, or something else explains the outcomes. Ask before choosing a remedy.

A new reviewer and a new but comparable case would help establish whether revised instructions work for an unfamiliar user. They would still not establish willingness to pay or reliability across every situation. Supplied observations are for critique, not entries to copy into your actual pilot record.

Carry forward: specify the invited population and observation window. Record the actual task, assistance, completion, defect, revision, and retest. Leave an event pending until it happens; retain failed attempts as evidence.

Prepare Milestone 3: pilot and learning

  1. Run the approved classroom or partner task with the versioned kit. Record date, user role, input, access, assistance, result, complete effort, and quality.
  2. Compare the observation with the baseline or explain why a fair numerical comparison is not available.
  3. Select one meaningful defect or uncertainty and revise the kit.
  4. Retest the affected path and check that an ordinary path still works. Keep the new version and observations.
  5. Write a proportionate continue/revise/stop decision and one alternative explanation.

Do not fill gaps with the supplied benchmark or AI-generated observations. If no other person has tried the kit, that requirement is pending. You can finish bench testing and prepare materials while arranging an appropriate reviewer. In self-paced study, invite a willing colleague or peer to perform the bounded task; no instructor access is implied.

For an instructor-led offering, the individual check near this milestone asks you to explain a calculation, a design choice, or an evidence limit using your own work. Keep the record concise enough that another person can inspect it. The next unit asks whether the observed gain could translate into sustainable business value.

Evidence and assessment criteria

Show the predeclared pilot question and measures, actual user-task record, defensible comparison, complete effort and quality, meaningful revision, and retest. Keep supplied benchmark figures separate from what happened with your version.

The result may support continuation, narrowing, or stopping. Grade quality of evaluation and judgment, not a positive AI result. Include the individual explanation near this milestone; together with the later check, it contributes to the individual component recorded under Milestone 5, not a second grade here.
