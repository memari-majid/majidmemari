Module 00

Orientation: Business Partner Capstone and AI Value

What you will be able to do

  • Trace a semester-adaptive capstone from a partner or classroom business need through design, implementation, controlled deployment, testing, and a sales or adoption pitch.
  • Connect a business measure to the problem, user, and decision it supports.
  • Scope a Finance, Marketing, MBA/strategy, MIS, or Operations solution to available evidence, data, tools, time, and partner access.

Seminar · understand the decision

Ideas to work with

Start with a business decision

The course is organized around a business capstone. Each semester, Memari selects or supplies a problem brief based on the partner’s need and the available data, tools, budget, skills, time, and access. Students discover the problem, design an AI-assisted solution, implement a minimum usable product or service with existing no-code tools, deploy it in a controlled setting, test it with a user, and pitch a supported sales or adoption decision. The core learning outcomes and rubrics stay stable while the project changes. Statistics, tables, and figures help judge the solution; they are not the final product by themselves. Published cases are practice options; follow Memari’s confirmed semester brief for the actual partner or classroom client.

Practical work without programming

Your capstone must do something useful for an intended user: a reviewed report or content kit, spreadsheet-supported briefing, customer-service pack, form-and-template process, or manually operated AI-assisted service. Ordinary documents, spreadsheets, forms, presentations, and available AI assistants are enough. Controlled deployment means making a versioned artifact available to a partner or classroom test user, checking access, and recording a real user task with permitted inputs. A public launch, custom software, paid tool, real sale, or payment is not required. A mockup alone is insufficient at the end; it must connect to a usable output or service that can be tested.

One coherent project, developed in stages

The five briefs provide practice and components for one capstone. The portfolio selects a feasible scope; the readiness plan and proposal define the pilot and offer; the final report and presentation demonstrate the implemented solution, deployment/user-test record, economics, operating plan, and handoff. Reuse earlier work within the existing page and slide limits. Majid Memari teaches and assesses the course. If partner access is unavailable, the instructor supplies the brief and a classroom proxy tests the solution under the same rubric. Use fictional, supplied, public non-sensitive, or specifically approved partner information, and distinguish actual classroom observations from scenario numbers.

A one-paragraph business brief

A useful starting brief might read: “Cedar Market staff repeatedly explain store policies while other customers wait. We propose investigating which questions recur, how often they require an exception, and whether clearer information would help. The decision is whether to improve the policy page, introduce reviewed AI-assisted drafts, or keep the current process. We would compare total staff effort, policy accuracy, and customer understanding.” Notice what this brief does not claim: it supplies no invented savings or demand estimate. It identifies a decision and the information needed to make it. Keep four headings in your working document—problem, alternatives, evidence, recommendation—and update them as the course progresses. A useful product or service begins with this brief, not with a list of tool features.

Module 01

AI Literacy and Effective Use

What you will be able to do

  • Brief a ready-made AI tool or evaluate a supplied output using explicit facts and constraints.
  • Check factual claims and calculate an accuracy-style rate with the correct denominator.
  • Explain why a headline percentage does not replace judgment about material errors.

Seminar · understand the decision

Ideas to work with

Capabilities and limits in business terms

AI tools can assist with drafting, summarizing, comparing, and generating alternatives. Their fluent output can also contain unsupported claims, omissions, or inconsistent reasoning. Treat an output as a draft to evaluate, not as proof. A useful business question is whether the output helps a person perform a task to an acceptable standard, with an appropriate review burden. You do not need to understand model engineering to make that judgment.

Brief the tool as you would brief a colleague

A useful request states the business task, relevant facts, intended audience, constraints, and desired format. For example: “Using only the fictional store policy below, draft a 100-word response to a customer asking about returns. If the policy does not answer a question, say what staff must confirm.” Then iterate: ask for two alternatives, compare them against your criteria, and request a targeted revision such as shorter wording or a clearer next step. Change one important instruction at a time so you can explain why the output improved. The same briefing process works when you draft manually. Longer prompts are not automatically better, and a detailed request cannot supply missing evidence. For an AI-assisted operating role, specify the input, task, output, evidence, acceptance check, and escalation rule. You might act as the coordinator of a research helper, draft helper, and review helper in separate chat sessions. These are bounded delegated tasks; they do not require autonomous software or remove your responsibility to approve the result.

Review before use

Judge a response against a simple checklist: Is it consistent with the supplied facts? Does it answer the customer’s actual question? Is its tone appropriate? Does it make promises the business cannot keep? Keep the original and revised outputs so you can explain your decisions. Disclose the tool and the part of the work it supported. If you use supplied outputs instead, document the same review process. For a numerical review, record both the number of supported claims and the number checked. Do not average percentages from differently sized groups unless equal group weighting is the question you intend to answer. Also identify the consequence of each important error: factual claims are not all equally consequential.

Worked example: from an unsafe draft to a usable response

Start with this fictional customer question: “I bought an unopened item 20 days ago and have the receipt. Can I return it?” A suitable task request is: “Using only the supplied Cedar Market policy, draft a concise answer. Preserve all eligibility conditions. Do not add promises about shipping, timing of payment, or exceptions. Identify any information staff must confirm.” A reviewed response is: “The policy allows returns of unopened items within 30 days with a receipt, so the situation you describe meets those listed conditions. Bring the item and receipt to store staff to confirm the return process.” The review record can be three lines: changed 60 days to 30; restored the unopened-item and receipt conditions; removed the unsupported shipping promise. This is an instructor-authored model response, not a claim that a particular AI tool produced it. You can evaluate and improve it manually.

Module 02

Business Problems and AI Opportunities

What you will be able to do

  • Frame a specific business problem and compare AI-assisted and non-AI alternatives.
  • Calculate and interpret a mean, median, and range in a small time table.
  • Estimate workload using explicit assumptions and explain sensitivity to unusual cases.

Seminar · understand the decision

Ideas to work with

Define the problem narrowly

“Improve the business” is too broad to guide a solution. A usable problem statement identifies the person, task, obstacle, and consequence: “Store staff spend time repeatedly answering return-policy questions, delaying other customer service.” Separate symptoms from causes. If the policy is unclear, an accurate policy page may help more than a new AI tool. The problem should be understandable without naming a technology. For a venture, ask who experiences the problem, who can pay, what they do now, and why changing would be worthwhile. For an internal project, identify the user and budget owner. A genuine need may be studied through public information or a permitted voluntary conversation, but the supplied fictional discovery packets are sufficient for the course.

Compare alternatives and the current process

Consider the status quo, a straightforward process improvement, and an AI-assisted option. A comparison should include expected benefit, effort, cost, quality risk, and staff responsibilities. AI does not need to win. Recommending a clearer form or FAQ can demonstrate strong business judgment. Avoid assuming every minute saved becomes a cash saving: time may be redirected to other work rather than removed from payroll.

Use modest, explicit evidence

A baseline describes current performance; a target describes the improvement you hope to achieve. For a fictional case, you may state that staff handle 40 policy questions per week and spend four minutes on each, provided these are labeled assumptions. Explain how the business could later verify them. Separate observations supplied by the case from your estimates and from possibilities suggested by AI. The mean is the total divided by the number of observations; the median is the middle of ordered observations, averaging the two middle values when needed. The range is the maximum minus the minimum. Use a small table or dot plot to see whether a long task changes the average. A convenient average from a few cases is not automatically a representative baseline.

Worked comparison: a simpler option can win

Use a clearly labeled planning scenario. Current work is 40 questions × 4 minutes = 160 minutes per week. Suppose a clearer FAQ reduces questions by 25%, with 10 minutes of weekly upkeep: 30 × 4 + 10 = 130 minutes, releasing 30 minutes. Suppose AI-assisted drafts take one minute to prepare plus two minutes to review for each of 40 questions, with 15 minutes of upkeep: 40 × 3 + 15 = 135 minutes, releasing 25 minutes. Under these assumptions, the FAQ releases slightly more time; neither result is observed. Setup effort, tool expense, accuracy, and customer access could change the decision. A defensible recommendation is to test the simpler FAQ first and reconsider AI if routine question volume remains high. The strongest evidence to collect is the actual reduction in questions and the complete review burden—not whether a demonstration looks impressive.

Module 03

Marketing and Sales

What you will be able to do

  • Connect an AI-assisted marketing artifact to a customer, offer, and measurable action.
  • Compare counts, response rates, percentage-point differences, and cost per inquiry.
  • Explain uncertainty and why a small uncontrolled comparison does not prove causation.

Seminar · understand the decision

Ideas to work with

A message needs a customer and a purpose

Marketing work begins with the audience, need, value proposition, and desired next action. “Create social media posts” specifies an output but not a business objective. A more useful brief might help first-time gift buyers understand a retailer’s offer. Give an AI assistant a small fact sheet and ask for alternatives emphasizing different benefits, then select and revise the strongest one. A message can connect a feature to a customer benefit—for example, local pickup may support convenient planning—but avoid claiming an outcome you have not established. Measure success against the intended customer action rather than the number of messages generated. Turn the brief into a small acquisition plan: one audience, one channel, one useful message, and one clear next action. The next action might be a qualified inquiry or a product walkthrough, not necessarily a purchase. Create the message in class; sending or publishing it is optional.

Brand quality and truthful claims

A polished message may still contain invented discounts, product features, testimonials, or guarantees. Review every factual or comparative claim against the fictional product brief. Avoid treating “best,” “guaranteed,” or “saves 50%” as harmless filler. Consistent tone, accessibility, and clear calls to action also matter. A business remains responsible for material it chooses to publish.

Measure business response

Output counts are easy to collect but rarely establish value. Compare measures such as qualified inquiries, completed purchases, or customer comprehension with costs and review time. A small concept test can ask classmates to interpret two fictional messages. Such feedback supports a claim about that exercise, not a claim that a real market has been validated. Report the scope and limits of the evidence. Compare like denominators. A rise from 4% to 5% is one percentage point and a 25% relative increase, not a one-percent relative improvement. Show the underlying counts because a high percentage from a small group can be unstable. Without comparable groups or a suitable test design, different results may reflect audience, timing, or other factors rather than the message. A simple funnel records people reached, qualified inquiries, proposals or trials, purchases, and repeat use. Define each stage before comparing rates. Customer acquisition cost is acquisition spending divided by new customers over a stated period, not spending divided by clicks. If no customers are acquired, report that fact rather than dividing by zero or inventing a return.

Worked artifact: a message, a claim audit, and a test

A grounded message is: “Make your weekend picnic easier with TrailBox: a $30 kit with reusable containers, available for local pickup. Ask about pickup arrangements.” Its claim audit is short: price, reusable containers, weekend use, and pickup come from the fictional brief; ease is a proposed benefit, not a measured result; delivery and environmental superiority are not supported. A second message could emphasize planning convenience without adding new product facts. For a classroom concept test, show both messages in a varied order and ask reviewers what the product costs, how they receive it, and what they think is included. Record misunderstandings and revise the message. Do not report a preference tally as a sales forecast. A real business would need suitable evidence about inquiries, purchases, acquisition cost, and fulfillment capacity before increasing campaign spending.

  • Calculate overall routing accuracy, missed-exception rate, and unnecessary-escalation rate from a count table.

Seminar · understand the decision

Ideas to work with

Design the customer experience, not only the reply

A service encounter includes what happens before, during, and after a response. Ask how the customer enters the process, what information they need, how they know the issue is resolved, and what happens if the response is wrong. AI may help draft or organize a response, while a person remains responsible for exceptions. Convenience for the business should not create more work for the customer. Delivery begins after the customer or internal sponsor accepts the offer. Define intake, preparation, approval, handoff, support, and follow-up. A useful deliverable should meet the promised scope and tell the recipient what to do next.

Define boundaries and escalation

Some questions can be answered from a clear policy; others require judgment, authorization, or information that is unavailable. A service concept should state what the assistant may answer, what it must not promise, and when a staff member takes over. A handoff is useful only if the customer knows what will happen next and the staff member receives enough context to help.

Balance speed with quality

Faster replies are not automatically better service. A useful evaluation includes policy accuracy, clear language, resolution, customer effort, and appropriate escalation. Consider both routine and exception cases. A storyboard or sample conversation can reveal problems before the business invests in a system. In this course, evaluating those artifacts is sufficient; a working chatbot is not required. Count missed exceptions separately from unnecessary escalations. The first uses all true exceptions as its denominator; the second uses all routine requests. Overall accuracy can look high when most requests are easy, even if a consequential minority is handled poorly. Plain-language error counts make the business tradeoff clearer than a single score. To study retention, define an eligible cohort and time window: for example, buyers with a full month in which to reorder. Repeat-purchase rate is repeat buyers divided by those eligible buyers, not repeat orders divided by every historical lead. For internal services, continued use and task completion may be more relevant than purchases.

Worked artifact: a useful handoff, not a false confirmation

An appropriate response is: “Regular check-in begins after 3 p.m. Early check-in depends on staff confirmation, so I cannot guarantee access at 9 a.m. You can ask the front desk to confirm whether an earlier room is available.” In a proposed service storyboard, the next scene can show a request routed to the front desk with the requested arrival time, the policy question, and the fact that no promise has been made. A quality review asks whether the guest understands the uncertainty, knows the next step, and receives a consistent answer. Do not invent luggage storage, compensation, or a response-time commitment absent from the policy.

Module 05

Business Knowledge and Decision Support

What you will be able to do

  • Use supplied business information to create an evidence-based decision brief.
  • Identify unsupported conclusions and missing information in a summary.
  • Communicate uncertainty and preserve responsibility for the decision.

Seminar · understand the decision

Ideas to work with

Summaries reduce reading, not responsibility

AI-assisted summaries can help a manager locate themes, compare documents, and prepare questions. They can also omit exceptions or present a tentative claim as certain. Keep the source material available and trace material conclusions back to it. The manager should be able to explain the recommendation without relying on the authority or confidence of the AI tool. The result can be a finished business product: a cash briefing, research summary, supplier comparison, or operational status pack. Specify the reader, decision, update frequency, source information, and review owner so it can be used consistently rather than remaining a one-off generated answer.

Separate facts, interpretations, and recommendations

A fact is explicitly supported by the supplied information. An interpretation explains what the facts may mean. A recommendation proposes action given an objective and tradeoffs. These are different kinds of statements. For example, a longer delivery time is a fact in a vendor table; calling a vendor “unreliable” may be an unsupported interpretation; choosing a backup supplier is a decision requiring context.

Communicate uncertainty usefully

A decision brief should state what is known, what is assumed, what remains unknown, and what evidence would change the recommendation. Avoid inventing a missing number to make a table look complete. If a business needs information that is unavailable, identify a practical way to obtain it. The best output may be a concise set of questions rather than an immediate answer.

Worked decision memo: urgency changes the comparison

Assume, solely for this example, an order of 100 units. North’s listed purchase cost is $1,200; Mesa’s is $1,400, a $200 premium for a listed delivery time three days shorter. These figures exclude shipping, defects, returns, and the consequence of delay. A concise memo might say: “For routine replenishment, North is the provisional lower-price option if seven-day delivery is acceptable. For an urgent order, Mesa may be worth the premium if its delivery commitment meets the need. We cannot establish superior reliability because Mesa’s defect rate is missing and North’s reported rate lacks comparison details. Confirm total delivered price, delivery commitment, and comparable quality information before committing.” Do not automatically multiply North’s 2% rate by the order and call the result a guaranteed loss. The reported rate may not predict this order, and the cost of a defect is unspecified.

A pilot is a learning plan

What you will be able to do

Seminar · understand the decision

Ideas to work with

Investigate the problem before selling the idea

Questions such as “Would you love an AI assistant?” invite agreeable but weak evidence. Ask how the person handles the task now, what is difficult, what consequences follow, and what alternatives they have tried. Avoid leading language and promises. In this course, classmate role-play or instructor-supplied case responses are sufficient; real customer outreach is not mandatory. After discovery, practice a short sales or internal-approval conversation: confirm the problem, ask about fit and constraints, show the artifact, explain scope and price or effort, respond to one objection, and agree on a next step. Do not force a close when the fit is weak. Role-play with the supplied responses is enough.

Interpret feedback within its limits

A role-play response, a fictional case, and a real interview are different evidence sources. Label each accurately. Record both favorable and unfavorable feedback and separate what the participant said from your interpretation. Three positive comments do not establish a market. Useful evidence may show that the problem is smaller than expected, the proposed experience is confusing, or a simpler solution is preferable. Always show a response count and denominator beside a percentage. One response moves a five-person group by 20 percentage points; a larger group is less sensitive to one response but can still be biased by recruitment or leading wording. Interest, an intention to buy, and an actual purchase are different outcomes. No formal statistical test is required to recognize these limits.

Revise a concrete concept

Use a storyboard, mockup, sample output, or manual service walkthrough to give feedback something specific to address. Ask what the reviewer thinks the product does, what is useful, what is unclear, and what would prevent adoption. Document one meaningful change and why it follows from the evidence. Use this feedback to strengthen the proposal and improve the usable business artifact; a custom application is unnecessary. Keep discovery and selling distinct: questions should first help you understand the problem; a later proposal presents a specific offer. A positive role-play reaction is not a commitment. Record what objection changed the offer and what evidence would justify a real launch or purchase. Refine the usable version and the planned deployment from this feedback. Discovery evidence can remain simulated, but the final user test must truthfully record someone using the implemented artifact in the approved partner or classroom setting.

Economics and Business Value

What you will be able to do

  • Calculate costs, capacity gains, and simple value estimates with visible units and periods.
  • Distinguish contribution, assumed economic value, and realized cash return.
  • Use a scenario table and threshold to explain uncertainty in the recommendation.

Seminar · understand the decision

Ideas to work with

Count all material costs

A business case includes more than a tool subscription. Consider setup effort, staff review, training, recurring use, support, and correction of mistakes. For a product or service, include the work needed to acquire and serve customers. Use a simple table and arithmetic rather than a complicated model. Label fictional figures and identify which assumptions have the greatest effect on the result. For an external offer, distinguish acquisition spending, variable delivery cost, and fixed operating costs. Contribution per order is price minus variable delivery cost; it must cover fixed costs and any excluded effort before there is profit. A repeat-customer assumption can change the economics, but a small fictional cohort does not justify a confident lifetime-value forecast.

Describe benefits honestly

Time saved can create capacity without reducing cash expenses. Revenue is the money received; profit depends on costs. Avoid counting the same benefit twice, such as both labor savings and extra output from the same hours. If benefits are difficult to monetize, state a relevant quality or service measure alongside the financial estimate. Do not force every improvement into an unsupported dollar figure.

Test the recommendation under uncertainty

A base estimate is only one possible outcome. Compare a lower-benefit or higher-cost case with your base case and explain what changes. Break-even can be expressed as the volume or benefit needed to cover costs. A recommendation becomes stronger when it states the conditions under which it would no longer make sense. Precision in arithmetic does not make uncertain assumptions certain. A return ratio is generally net benefit divided by the relevant investment or cost, with a clearly stated period and included items. If benefits are only assigned values for released staff capacity, label the ratio accordingly; it is not realized cash ROI. A simple table separating recurring cash changes, capacity value, and one-time effort is more informative than an impressive unlabeled percentage.

Worked economics: capacity, cash, and sensitivity

With 100 listings monthly, manual work is 100 × 12 ÷ 60 = 20 hours. AI-assisted drafting plus five-minute review is 100 × (3 + 5) ÷ 60 = 13.33 hours. The scenario releases 6.67 hours, valued at $160 using the assumed $24 hourly rate. Subtracting the $60 tool expense gives $100 of illustrative net capacity value, not demonstrated cash profit: payroll is unchanged and the business must use the released time productively. Setup is four hours, valued at $96, shown separately as a one-time effort. If review takes eight minutes, the process uses 18.33 hours; released capacity is worth only $40, below the $60 expense. Under the stated recurring assumptions, capacity value equals tool cost at 7.5 minutes of review. This threshold helps identify the measurement that matters; it is not a promise of savings.

Understand the problem

Cedar Services is a fictional small business whose owner receives a cash schedule but struggles to identify timing problems before making routine operating commitments.   • User: owner or operations manager making routine planning decisions.
  • Preparer: finance assistant maintaining the fictional schedule.
  • Reviewer and internal sponsor: finance manager or owner.

Discover the need

Supplied evidence

  • Supplied fictional owner response: “The monthly total looks fine, but I need to know whether a particular week is tight.”
  • Supplied fictional assistant response: “Checking that the narrative matches the schedule takes longer than formatting it.”
  • Supplied fictional manager response: “Keep uncertain receipts visible; do not turn a forecast into a promise.”

Questions to investigate

  • What decision does the owner need to make each week?
  • Which receipt or payment dates are least certain, and why?
  • What would a useful brief add beyond the existing spreadsheet?

Design the AI-assisted solution

  1. Record the supplied opening cash, receipts, payments, and uncertainty labels in an ordinary table or spreadsheet.
  2. Calculate each closing balance and carry it into the next week.
  3. Use an AI assistant or manual drafting to summarize the low point, assumptions, and follow-up questions.
  4. Check every number and statement against the schedule before delivering the brief.

The role of AI

Draft a plain-language narrative, identify statements that need verification, and propose questions about timing. It does not certify the forecast or decide what payments to make.

A reasonable non-AI alternative

A reusable cash-summary template completed and checked manually.

Human review

A person verifies balances, dates, reserve comparisons, and the distinction between cash and profit. Missing or disputed inputs are escalated rather than invented.

  • A reusable prompt or drafting checklist and a human-approval line.

Acceptance checks

  • All balances reconcile and the closing balance carries forward correctly.

What counts as implementation and deployment?

  • Deploy a minimum usable solution that a test user can access and use for the agreed task. Examples include a shared document product, spreadsheet-supported briefing, form-and-template process, reviewed content/customer-service kit, or a manually operated AI-assisted service.
  • A private classroom delivery is valid: provide a dated/versioned file or approved shared location, verify the recipient can open it, and observe or record a defined user task. A public website, app store, payment system, custom code, or live integration is not required.
  • A standalone pitch deck, attractive mockup, or plan is not sufficient for the final capstone. A prototype interface must connect to a usable manual deliverable or service process that can be tested end to end.
  • Keep a short deployment and test record: version, location/delivery method, user role, task, access check, actual result, relevant quality/time measures, feedback, revision, and remaining limits. Fictional inputs are allowed; do not invent the user-test outcome.
  • Obtain the partner’s agreement before external use of its workflow, data, brand, or customer-facing material; use the instructor-approved classroom route if approval or access is unavailable.
  • Use human review for final claims, decisions, and exceptions. Define a stop/fallback step and a handoff owner. An AI-agent role is a bounded delegated task, not permission for unsupported autonomous actions.

If no partner is available, access is delayed, or the scope exceeds available resources, Memari supplies a fictional or public-information brief and acts as sponsor. A classmate or instructor serves as the test user. Students still implement a usable artifact, make it available in a private classroom setting, run a real classroom user task with supplied inputs, and document the outcome. 07

Onboard and deliver with a clear AI-assisted process

  1. Scope: the owner confirms the five products, deliverables, price, revision boundary, and timing.
  2. Inputs: the customer supplies approved product facts and confirms permission to use the material. Missing facts stay missing until verified.
  3. Draft: an AI drafting role produces structured copy from the approved facts only.
  4. Review: an AI checking role flags unsupported claims; the owner independently checks the facts and arithmetic.
  5. Delivery: the owner approves the editable pack and the handoff note. The customer decides what to publish.
  6. Follow-up: ask what required revision, whether the pack was used, and whether another batch would be useful.

Track on-time accepted batches, rework minutes, important errors, complaints, and repeat orders. A second purchase or repeated use tells you something different from initial interest. A tiny repeat-customer sample still cannot support a broad retention claim.
