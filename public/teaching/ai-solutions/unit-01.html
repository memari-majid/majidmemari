Unit 01 of 12

AI and a useful business task

What can AI contribute, and what must a person verify?

Learn

By the end of this unit

  • Distinguish an AI output from evidence about the business.
  • Repair a reply using approved sources and explain the human decision.

Begin with the work someone needs done

Cedar Desk needs an accurate, useful reply to a booking question. “Use AI” does not define success. The coordinator must understand the request, find the right policy, explain the conditions, and avoid promising an action that has not happened. A tool is useful only when it improves that complete task enough to justify its effort and cost.

Generative AI produces a draft, summary, or proposed explanation. Prediction or classification estimates a quantity or assigns a category, such as “needs owner review.” A workflow follows specified steps. An agent can choose among actions toward a goal, within the access and authority it has been given. These are different capabilities. Fluent wording does not prove a prediction is accurate, and giving a tool an agent label does not authorize it to change a booking.

Your continuing product, ReplyKit, is a small service another coordinator can operate. It includes an intake sheet, sources, a drafting instruction, review criteria, exception handling, and a delivery record. A manually operated kit is a valid implementation. The final demonstration must show a user doing a task, not only an attractive interface.

See a task brief you can copy and improve
Using only sources S1–S6 in the Cedar Desk packet, prepare a concise reply to this fictional booking question. List the source for each material claim. If a needed fact is missing, ask for it. Do not promise availability, policy exceptions, or actions already completed. Return a draft, a source table, and any item needing human review.

This instruction supplies a task, evidence boundary, audience, format, and stop condition. It reduces ambiguity; it does not guarantee correct output. Keep the original draft so you can show what you checked and changed.

Repair a confident but unsupported answer

Question: “I want to move my booking. The session is in 60 hours.” The supplied AI draft says: “Your place has been moved, and there is no charge. Your new session is confirmed.” Before reading the explanation, mark each claim supported, unsupported, or unknown.

Worked review

The timing meets the at-least-48-hour condition in S3, but availability and the requested destination are unknown. No booking action occurred. “Moved,” “no charge,” and “confirmed” are unsupported. A sound revision is: “A change can be considered because your session is at least 48 hours away. It depends on available space and coordinator confirmation. Which session would you like us to check? Your booking has not yet been changed.”

The correction is a business decision: preserve the customer’s next step without creating a false commitment. Counting three polished sentences would not reveal that problem.

Now change the request to 20 hours before the session. The 48-hour rule no longer applies. The reply should identify owner review, not simply reuse the earlier answer. A good test varies a consequential fact.

Your continuing case: Cedar Desk

Fictional teaching case. Cedar Desk is a booking and customer-support service for small workshop businesses. Its coordinators answer questions about bookings, cancellations, rescheduling, and special requests. The operations owner wants dependable replies without unnecessary checking or repeated clarification. Your project is ReplyKit: a small source-grounded reply service that turns a permitted question into a checked response or a useful human handoff. It can run with documents, a calculator, and an available AI assistant or the supplied draft. No application or live customer account is needed.

The user is a booking coordinator. The buyer or internal sponsor is the operations owner. A customer receives the final approved reply. Those are three different roles. The owner has agreed only to a classroom exploration in this fictional case, not to a purchase, a live integration, or a commercial launch. Your instructor may replace this packet with an approved partner brief while preserving the same tasks and evidence standards.

Discovery packet

Eight supplied, fictional interview records describe waits of 4, 5, 5, 6, 6, 7, 8, and 31 minutes for a response. Six of eight describe repeated delay; three of eight say they would try a pilot. These convenience-case records are teaching inputs, not a representative market survey. They do not establish purchase intent. Response waiting time is different from the staff processing time in the later benchmark.

  • Coordinator: “I often know the basic answer. Finding the latest policy and asking about exceptions takes longer.”
  • Customer: “My last reply was quick, but I still did not know whether the date change was confirmed.”
  • Owner: “I might try a small test if I can inspect every reply. I have not approved a budget.”
  • Contrary evidence: Two of the eight records report no repeated delay. One of these customers prefers a clear policy page to another conversation.

These are invented case statements, not quotations from real people. In a discovery role-play, the respondent uses the card and says “unknown” when the packet does not answer a question. Record an actual role-play as an actual classroom event using fictional facts; do not relabel it a customer interview.

A complete usable example

Intake: Case 5; request to move a booking 60 hours before the session. Sources: S3, S4, S6. Draft: “Your request is at least 48 hours before the session, so a change can be considered. It depends on available space and coordinator confirmation. Which session would you like us to check? Your booking has not yet been changed.” Review: Timing, conditional wording, missing desired session, and no false confirmation checked. Handoff: A coordinator checks availability and confirms or declines the requested change. Status: Approved draft for classroom use; no external message sent.

Make the example usable by another person: provide this source card, an intake sheet, a draft-and-source table, the review checklist, an exception note, and a delivery record. Number the version and label who performs each step. A slide showing those items without providing them is not a working kit.

Start one project record

Create a folder or document titled ReplyKit. Add five sections: need and evidence; solution and workflow; tests and revision; economics and offer; delivery and handoff. You will improve these sections throughout the course rather than write a new project for every lesson.

  1. Write the user, task, and intended result in one sentence.
  2. Save the supplied draft, your revised answer, and a three-row source/claim check.
  3. Record what AI contributed, what you checked, and the decision you retained.
  4. Write one uncertainty about the business need. Do not invent an interview to resolve it.

Use an available AI assistant with fictional facts or analyze the supplied draft. Both routes practice the same judgment. Save the task instruction and supplied-output label when using the latter. In the next unit, you investigate whether the problem behind the reply is worth solving.

What will you carry forward?

A draft correctly states the policy but says a booking has been changed. No action occurred. What is the best revision?

In your project record, state what you decided, the evidence you used, and one uncertainty to test next. Explain the reasoning in your own words.
