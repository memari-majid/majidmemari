Three practical capstone paths

Look closely at the artifact.

These original classroom examples illustrate scope and quality. They use fictional or supplied facts. They are learning examples, not evidence that an actual business has achieved the stated outcome.

Example artifact · fictional Cedar Market

A usable digital product kit

The business needs consistent answers to routine returns questions. The product is a small, reviewed information pack that staff can use immediately—not a proposed chatbot.

CEDAR MARKET / RETURNS REPLY KIT

A clear answer, every time.

Approved case facts: returns are accepted for unopened items within 30 days with a receipt. The case does not specify shipping refunds or exceptions.

Thanks for contacting Cedar Market. Our policy allows returns of unopened items within 30 days with a receipt. Please bring the item and receipt so our team can review the return. If your item has been opened, is outside that period, or you have a question about shipping, a staff member will need to confirm what options are available.

Included in the kit

  • The reviewed customer response above.
  • A short FAQ separating known policy from questions requiring staff review.
  • A staff checklist: unopened? within 30 days? receipt? exception?

Why this is useful

The artifact is useful because it is specific, accurate to the supplied policy, and easy to review. Its professional tone does not establish that customers will prefer it; that would require feedback.

What to test next

Test the kit against routine and exception scenarios. Record incorrect promises, escalation accuracy, and staff review time.

Example service · fictional retail operations

An AI-assisted service with a finished sample

Offer a weekly customer-question insight brief. AI can help group supplied questions; a person checks counts, removes identifying details, and approves the recommendations.

WEEKLY CUSTOMER-QUESTION BRIEF / CLASSROOM EXAMPLE

Help customers find the answer earlier.

Fictional sample of 20 supplied questions
TopicCountSuggested business action
Return policy10Make the approved policy easier to find.
Opening hours6Check that the website and entrance display agree.
Stock availability4Route availability questions to staff with current information.

Recommendation: test a clearer policy link first. These invented classroom counts illustrate the service format; they are not measured customer research.

Review process: inspect source questions → verify grouping and totals → check each proposed action → approve the brief.

Why this is useful

The service has a defined input, a finished output, a review responsibility, and a repeatable process. It is more concrete than promising “AI-powered insights.”

What to test next

In a real pilot, use authorized data and compare the brief with a manual version. Measure usefulness and the total preparation-plus-review time.

Example experience · fictional gift-selection service

From storyboard to a usable no-code service

The storyboard defines the user journey. A shared request sheet, a reviewed shortlist, and a staff handoff turn it into a working manual service that can be tested without programming.

GIFT SHORTLIST / FOUR-SCREEN CONCEPT

  1. 01

    Tell us the occasion

    “A thank-you gift for a friend who likes cooking.”

  2. 02

    Set your boundaries

    Budget, timing, and preferences. Avoid requesting sensitive information.

  3. 03

    Review a shortlist

    Show sample options with the known product facts and clear reasons for the match.

  4. 04

    Confirm with staff

    Check stock, current price, and pickup arrangements before making a promise.

A usable classroom implementation

Supplied fictional inventory: navy ceramic mug $14; recipe journal $9; cooking class $60. Availability, tax, and shipping are not supplied.

Request-sheet input: a thank-you gift for someone who likes cooking; item budget $25.

Reviewed output: the mug and recipe journal total $23 in item prices, leaving $2 of the stated item budget. The class exceeds the budget. Confirm availability and any additional costs with staff before purchase.

Delivery process: collect the request in a shared sheet or form, draft the shortlist with a ready-made assistant or the supplied output, verify every item and sum, deliver the reviewed shortlist to the user, and record whether it supports the gift-selection task.

Why this is useful

The storyboard explains the experience; the implemented request-and-shortlist process delivers something a user can use. One successful classroom task would show limited usability evidence, not market demand or production reliability.

What to test next

Give a peer the request sheet and deliver the reviewed shortlist. Observe task completion, confusion, total time, and needed corrections; record actual feedback as a classroom pilot using fictional data. Revise and retest, then hand off the instructions and quality checklist.
