Find users and sell or secure adoption

How does a prospect or sponsor reach a justified next step?

By the end of this unit

  • Connect one audience and channel to a meaningful next action.
  • Use funnel denominators and acquisition cost to evaluate an honest offer conversation.

Choose a reachable audience and a useful next step

A strong offer can still fail if it never reaches the person who needs or authorizes it. Choose one audience before drafting messages. For ReplyKit, that could be operations owners of small workshop services who manage repeated policy questions. “Everyone who likes AI” is not a useful segment. An internal initiative similarly needs a sponsor and a practical route to the coordinators who will use it.

A channel is a way to reach that audience: a relevant professional group, an existing customer relationship, an approved event, or a scheduled internal briefing. Explain why the audience is present and why your proposed contact is appropriate. This course requires a message and a credible plan; it does not require sending unsolicited messages or buying advertising.

In a fictional campaign, 100 relevant prospects were reached, 20 had qualified conversations, and 5 agreed to a pilot next step. A pilot agreement is not a payment or a completed delivery. Explore the linked counts below.

The conversation rate is 20/100 =20%. The pilot-next-step rate among conversations is 5/20 =25%. Across everyone reached, it is 5/100 =5%. Define “qualified” before counting—for example, the person owns the relevant problem and can discuss its consequences. Counting every click as a qualified lead would inflate performance.

Customer acquisition cost uses new paying customers when it claims to measure that quantity. Suppose outreach uses $150 cash and five hours valued at $24/hour: $270 including time. If all five pilot prospects later become new paying customers, acquisition cost is $54 each. If only three pay, it is $90. The current funnel alone does not establish either outcome. The provider’s $60 contribution before acquisition in Unit 9 becomes $6 at $54 acquisition cost, or − $30 at $90, before fixed development.

Keep the people—and the denominator—in view

Before counting customers, decide which event actually demonstrates payment.

The $270 acquisition effort includes $150 cash and 5 hours valued at $24/hour. Three paying customers imply $90 each, leaving−$30 after the offer’s $60 contribution before acquisition. If all five agreements become paying customers, acquisition is $54 and contribution is $6 each. Neither scenario includes fixed development. Payment and completed delivery remain different events.

Use it in your project: Label the stages, denominator, time period, and acquisition effort for your own channel. Keep interest separate from payment or approval.

Rehearse a conversation that can end with “not yet”

Use a classmate or the supplied owner role. Begin with discovery: “Which booking questions create extra work?” Clarify the present method and the cost of the problem. Then offer the bounded kit, explain the evidence, address an objection, and ask for a proportionate next step.

Original worked conversation

Owner: “Why should I pay when the current process works?” Learner: “You may not need this. The supplied benchmark suggests a small effort reduction with review, but our monthly base scenario does not show positive net capacity value. We can first test whether the kit improves consistency on your approved cases. Would a bounded classroom demonstration and a review of the full costs help you decide?”

Owner: “I do not want a system sending replies alone.” Learner: “The offered version prepares drafts and exception notes. Your reviewer retains approval. Booking changes and customer sending are excluded.” Next step: Agree only on a demonstration, its evidence, and who will review it. Do not record a sale or approval that did not occur.

Five simultaneous orders would exceed the example provider’s three-package monthly capacity. A truthful conversation must disclose scheduling or reduce commitments. A successful sales funnel is not a successful delivery system unless the work can be served.

Prepare Milestone 4: viability and route to users

Choose one audience and one channel. Write a short approved-fact message, a useful call to action, and a funnel with clearly named stages. Mark all unobserved counts as assumptions. Define which stage actually demonstrates use, approval, or payment. Include acquisition effort, even if the channel has no cash fee.

Rehearse the offer with an objection and a possible refusal. Record what the respondent actually challenged and how you changed the offer or next question. Label a role-play accurately. Add onboarding: who receives the kit, how they access it, which first task they perform, where help comes from, and when success is checked.

Submit or review the economics, capacity, offer, channel, conversation, and onboarding as one milestone snapshot. Do not copy the entire earlier dossier into a new paper. Explain why the proposed next step is justified by the evidence rather than by enthusiasm for the tool. Next, make the service dependable after the initial demonstration.

Evidence and assessment criteria

Update the project record with economics, capacity, offer, acquisition or internal-adoption route, a sales/approval conversation, and onboarding. One coherent internal or external context is sufficient. Do not create another venture alongside the existing capstone.

Separate observation from forecast, identify missing costs, and explain the decision under a less favorable scenario. A role-play can demonstrate the conversation; actual outreach, payment, or a positive commercial result is not required.
