Shape the offer

Who pays or approves, what is included, and why choose this?

Learn

By the end of this unit

  • Write a truthful offer that matches the usable solution and its evidence.
  • Explain price, delivery cost, capacity, and the buyer’s alternative together.

Sell a defined result, not an unrestricted promise

An offer tells a buyer or sponsor what they receive, for whom, under what conditions, and at what price or resource commitment. Cedar Desk does not buy “AI transformation.” It might approve a bounded reply-kit pilot with a source card, reviewed examples, training, and an evidence report. That promise must match the version you can deliver.

The internal case and a potential service business use the same reasoning. The operations owner can authorize staff time and a pilot budget; another workshop operator might later buy a setup service. Neither interest nor classroom success establishes willingness to pay. Treat an external offer as a hypothesis until appropriate evidence supports it.

For a provider-side planning example, allow 3 hours to adapt the kit,1 hour to review, and 0.5 hour to hand off:4.5 hours. At an assigned $24/hour, that is $108 of owner time, plus $12 incremental tool expense, or $120 cost including valued time. The $180 price leaves $60 contribution after that time valuation, before acquisition and fixed development. Cash left before compensating owner time is different: $168. Neither number is complete profit.

If the provider has 15 delivery hours a month,4.5 hours per package permits three whole packages, not 3.33 completed deliveries. Those units produce at most $180 contribution after valued time and before acquisition/fixed costs. A large audience does not remove this capacity limit.

Try

Correct an offer that outruns the evidence

The proposed headline says: “Save 75% of labor costs with automatic customer service.” What supports it? The unchecked benchmark uses 75% less supplied processing time but has three material errors; it does not measure labor expense. The reviewed benchmark shows 12.5% less complete effort in that set. Neither supports an automatic-service or guaranteed-savings promise.

A more credible offer and objection response

“A reviewed reply-kit pilot for one workshop policy. Your coordinator keeps control of commitments. We will document task quality, complete effort, and the conditions for continued use.” This describes what the buyer actually gets.

Objection: “We can use a template ourselves.” Response: “That may be sufficient. We can compare your template with the reviewed kit on the same cases. The question is whether the extra adaptation and review produce enough useful improvement to justify the cost.” The response respects the alternative instead of inventing a reason that AI must win.

At $60 contribution per package, an assumed $240 fixed development effort requires four packages to recover on this value basis, before acquisition cost. Do not silently convert this into a four-month forecast; the sales rate and capacity remain uncertain.

Add an offer the recipient can evaluate

Write one offer card using your actual project: user, buyer/sponsor, useful result, included work, exclusions, price or resource request, delivery conditions, acceptance task, support, and next step. Use an internal resource request when no sale is intended; the buyer still needs to understand the commitment and expected benefit.

Place the strongest evidence beside each material claim. Keep uncertain benefits conditional. Check the promise against your operating workflow and capacity model. A service that requires human review must not be described as fully autonomous.

Explain why the buyer might choose the current process instead. Record one question about willingness to adopt or pay that your classroom work has not answered. The next unit uses the offer in a specific channel and a conversation that can lead to a justified next step.
