Design a fair test

What would count as improvement, unacceptable quality, or an inconclusive result?

Learn

By the end of this unit

  • Compare policies on the same cases using quality and complete effort.
  • Write a bounded test with predeclared measures, decision rules, and evidence limits.

Planned guided work including textbook reading: 83–133 minutes, plus project work. This is a design estimate.

Compare the complete task on a common basis

A fast draft and a finished reply are different outputs. A useful comparison gives each method the same cases, sources, and completion criteria. Record preparation, checking, correction, and handoff, not only typing. Define what counts as an acceptable final reply before seeing which method is fastest.

The unchecked method is fastest but creates three material errors. The reviewed method releases 96 − 84 =12 minutes, or 12 ÷ 96 =12.5% of the manual total, in this supplied case set. Average complete effort falls from 8 to 7 minutes per reply. At an assumed $24/hour, twelve minutes has an assigned capacity value of $4.80. Unchanged payroll means no demonstrated cash saving, and tool costs remain separate.

Set a quality boundary and a value question. A possible classroom decision rule is no unsupported commitments in the observed cases and at least 10% lower complete effort. These are chosen teaching thresholds, not universal standards. A serious failure should trigger review even when the average score looks good. Record unresolved or skipped cases as such; never count them as successful.

Why zero observed errors is not proof of zero risk

A small supplied case set can omit rare or unfamiliar situations. Even in a simplified model with independent, representative cases and a true 10% error chance, seeing zero errors in 12 trials has probability 0.9 raised to 12, approximately 28%. You do not need this formula to make the decision: zero observed errors supports a bounded next test, not a guarantee about every future customer. The actual classroom cases are not automatically a random sample.

7.4: Overfitting: introductory example

David L. Poole and Alan K. Mackworth. Artificial Intelligence: Foundations of Computational Agents. 3rd edition. Cambridge University Press, 2023.

Read: Read the opening explanation and Example 7.15 about restaurant ratings. Stop before the next discussion of model complexity and Example 7.16. The polynomial example and equations are not assigned.

Why this unit: Provides an accessible example of why a result based on a small or favorable sample can disappoint later.

Course connection: Compare the current approach with the AI-assisted approach on comparable tasks using the same scoring rules. Keep some new cases for a later retest. Random assignment or alternating task order can reduce particular sources of bias, but a small classroom pilot does not guarantee balanced groups or prove a general causal effect. Record limitations instead of claiming certainty.

Course connection: The book discusses learned models. Apply its caution to testing your solution: repeatedly tuning instructions to familiar examples can make those examples look successful without showing that the solution handles new cases. Prompt revision is not training the underlying AI model. Keep some comparable cases out of development and use the same scoring rule when you later test them.

Does the reviewed process still meet the declared rule?

The fixed challenge keeps the twelve cases, manual comparison, and quality counts unchanged. It adds six hypothetical rework minutes to the reviewed process. This is a sensitivity exercise, not another observed experiment. The classroom effort limit is 96 × (1 − 0.10) = 86.4 minutes; a result at or below that limit meets the at-least-10% rule.

The unchecked process takes 24 minutes but leaves three material errors, so it fails the quality boundary. Design a fairer actual comparison

If one person repeats identical questions, the second method may benefit from memory. Use matched task versions or vary the order across reviewers when feasible, record the order, and keep instructions and quality criteria consistent. Small classroom tests still have limits. Describe them instead of claiming that a before-and-after difference proves AI caused every improvement.

Carry forward: state the capstone task, common cases, acceptable-output definition, complete timing, reviewer, and continue/revise/stop rule before your next comparison. Name the unfamiliar cases and operating costs that still need evidence.

Compare with the worked reasoning

Garden exchange’s location is “Not supplied”; Library Room 2 belongs only to Repair meetup. Preserve 15 May as stated and do not invent a year. Reviewed extraction uses 68 rather than 80 minutes: 12 minutes less, or 12/80 = 15%, with 12/12 acceptable rows in this supplied set. It meets both chosen classroom rules. Fast extraction uses 90 minutes and leaves three unacceptable rows. Eight added repair minutes raise the reviewed total to 76: (80 − 76)/80 = 5%, missing the 10% effort rule even if quality stays 12/12. These are the extraction task’s times, not the reply-policy table’s times. Retain a manual fallback and revise or investigate another justified benefit. Twelve fictional cases cannot establish future reliability; keep input cases, quality definitions, assistance and timing consistent.
