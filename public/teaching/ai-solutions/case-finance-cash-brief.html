Build a Weekly Cash-Briefing Kit

Help the owner understand projected cash timing, the assumptions behind it, and the questions requiring attention. A clear numerical schedule and source-checked narrative are more useful than a confident AI summary that confuses cash with profit.

Fictional supplied case · optional application

Understand the problem

Cedar Services is a fictional small business whose owner receives a cash schedule but struggles to identify timing problems before making routine operating commitments. A finance assistant spends time turning the schedule into an understandable briefing. This pathway can be an internal finance improvement or a hypothetical reviewed reporting service; it does not involve investment recommendations, credit decisions, tax advice, or movement of money. This illustrative alternative uses the same milestones as the semester capstone. The confirmed brief and available resources determine the final scope. Complete a controlled partner or classroom deployment of the usable kit and record an actual user task.

Help the owner understand projected cash timing, the assumptions behind it, and the questions requiring attention. A clear numerical schedule and source-checked narrative are more useful than a confident AI summary that confuses cash with profit.

People involved

  • User: owner or operations manager making routine planning decisions.
  • Preparer: finance assistant maintaining the fictional schedule.
  • Reviewer and internal sponsor: finance manager or owner.

Discover the need

Supplied evidence

  • Supplied fictional owner response: “The monthly total looks fine, but I need to know whether a particular week is tight.”
  • Supplied fictional assistant response: “Checking that the narrative matches the schedule takes longer than formatting it.”
  • Supplied fictional manager response: “Keep uncertain receipts visible; do not turn a forecast into a promise.”
  • The case supplies a four-week cash schedule and an $8,000 minimum planning reserve. It supplies no verified customer willingness to pay for an external service.

Questions to investigate

  • What decision does the owner need to make each week?
  • Which receipt or payment dates are least certain, and why?
  • What would a useful brief add beyond the existing spreadsheet?

Design the AI-assisted solution

  1. Record the supplied opening cash, receipts, payments, and uncertainty labels in an ordinary table or spreadsheet.
  2. Calculate each closing balance and carry it into the next week.
  3. Use an AI assistant or manual drafting to summarize the low point, assumptions, and follow-up questions.
  4. Check every number and statement against the schedule before delivering the brief.

The role of AI

Draft a plain-language narrative, identify statements that need verification, and propose questions about timing. It does not certify the forecast or decide what payments to make.

A reasonable non-AI alternative

A reusable cash-summary template completed and checked manually.

Human review

A person verifies balances, dates, reserve comparisons, and the distinction between cash and profit. Missing or disputed inputs are escalated rather than invented.

Build and deliver an artifact

Weekly Cash-Briefing Kit

One-page briefing plus a simple cash schedule and a short assumption/check log, created with ordinary document or spreadsheet tools.

What the usable package contains

  • A four-week schedule with opening cash, receipts, payments, closing cash, and comparison with the planning reserve.
  • A concise narrative naming the lowest projected balance, the week it occurs, and the assumptions that matter.
  • A reusable prompt or drafting checklist and a human-approval line.

Acceptance checks

  • All balances reconcile and the closing balance carries forward correctly.
  • The brief identifies the Week 2 reserve shortfall and labels the numbers as a scenario.
  • The late-receipt variation is reflected in the correct week, without changing the total receipt amount.
  • No claim equates projected cash with profit or implies that an actual payment was authorized.

Business numbers · interpret the evidence

Cash timing, not just the month-end total

Read the numbers before deciding

  1. Recalculate the four closing balances.
  2. Which week falls below the $8,000 reserve, and by how much?
  3. If $2,000 of Week 3 receipts arrive in Week 4 instead, what changes?
Compare your reasoning with the worked answer

Balances are $10,000+$6,000−$5,000=$11,000; then $7,000, $8,000, and $9,000. Week 2 is $1,000 below the planning reserve. Delaying $2,000 from Week 3 to Week 4 gives Week 3 closing cash of $6,000 and Week 4 still $9,000. The lowest point becomes $6,000, a $2,000 reserve gap. The month-end total is unchanged, but the timing risk is different. These are forecast scenarios, not realized cash or profit.

Run a bounded pilot

Can the kit produce a clearer, correct briefing with less total preparation and review effort than the current template?

Plan

  • Construct the actual schedule and briefing from the supplied packet.
  • Test three cases: the base schedule; $2,000 of Week 3 receipts delayed to Week 4; an additional fictional $1,500 Week 2 payment.
  • Check the resulting balances and narrative against manual arithmetic, recording errors and one revision.
  • If timing the classroom process, record preparation plus checking and describe the exercise as classroom evidence.
  • Make the versioned kit available to an approved partner or classroom user, verify access, record a defined task and actual feedback, revise, and provide a short handoff. This is the required controlled deployment; no public release is necessary.

Success measures

  • Correct calculations and no omitted below-reserve week in the three supplied tests.
  • A reader can identify the lowest projected balance and its uncertainty.
  • Any time-reduction claim includes review and is based on an actual labeled classroom comparison or remains a hypothesis.
  • A partner or classroom recipient can access the artifact and complete the agreed task; the record accurately describes the outcome, including failures.

When to pause or stop

  • An approved brief contains a wrong balance or an unsupported payment instruction.
  • The assistant cannot preserve the distinction between assumptions, actual cash, and profit.

Make the adoption or business decision

Value inside an organization

A more usable planning brief and potentially lower preparation/review effort. Payroll savings and avoided losses are not established by the scenario.

A possible venture

A hypothetical cash-reporting template or reviewed briefing service for small businesses, with tightly defined scope and human review. Prepare an offer and acquisition plan in role-play; no real sale or regulated financial service is required.

Assumptions to test

  • Whether the brief addresses an important recurring need.
  • Complete staff effort and any tool expense.
  • Who approves spending and what support or revision is included.
  • Whether a simpler manual template provides the same value.
