What you will be able to do

  • Translate the agreed business need into a minimum usable product or service.
  • Construct a document, spreadsheet, form/template, or manually operated AI-assisted solution with ordinary tools.
  • Test the artifact on three supplied cases, revise it, and identify what remains before a controlled user deployment.

Seminar · understand the decision

Ideas to work with

Describe the value before the features

A digital product can be a reusable document kit, a content pack, a decision aid, or a proposed app experience. An AI-enabled service can deliver a finished research brief, reviewed marketing package, or customer-response set. Describe the user, the job they need done, the useful output they receive, and what makes the output ready to use. Select one central use case. For ListingLift, the product is not merely “AI writes text”; it is a retailer receiving a clear listing with its claims checked against the product facts. The business value could come from consistency, convenience, quality, or reduced effort, and each is a hypothesis to evaluate.

Make the idea concrete without building software

Choose the smallest implementation that completes a useful task. A document product can contain three reviewed pieces; a reporting kit can calculate and explain a supplied table; a service can turn an intake brief into a checked deliverable; a simple form/template process can collect inputs and guide a manual handoff. Ordinary no-code tools are sufficient. A slide prototype can help explain an interface, but the final capstone must connect it to a usable deliverable or service. Build the artifact itself and test a routine request, a missing-information case, and an exception. Record actual classroom observations and one revision without calling them market validation.

Test understanding before claiming demand

A concept review can ask whether the intended user understands the offer, what problem it solves, and what concerns remain. Positive comments do not establish willingness to pay or long-term demand. A useful test has a question, a specific artifact, a limited audience, and a record of what changed. Classmate role-play or supplied case responses can substitute for external outreach. An offer card can state: “For [user], we deliver [specific useful result], including [scope], through [process], with [review/limit].” If you are exploring a venture, add a hypothetical price and next step; an internal initiative can use a resource request. Make it clear what is promised and what is not included.

Worked product sample: ListingLift without an application

Use this fictional fact sheet: “Cedar ceramic mug; 12-ounce capacity; $20; dishwasher-safe; local pickup.” An unsafe draft says: “Handmade from 100% recycled materials, this unbreakable mug includes free delivery.” None of those additions is supported. A reviewed sample is: “Cedar Ceramic Mug — $20. A 12-ounce ceramic mug that is dishwasher-safe and available for local pickup. Ask the shop about current availability.” The product concept is a small, usable listing kit: an input fact sheet, a reviewed listing, and a claim-check note. Its storyboard shows the retailer supplying facts, receiving a draft, reviewing claims, and approving the final version. This example demonstrates a possible service output and review process. It does not prove that the product is automated, that customers will pay, or that it saves time. A finished document kit can be a valid course artifact even when no app exists.

Carry this into your decision

A tangible concept shows what goes in, what the user receives, how quality is checked, and what useful outcome follows. A polished product name alone does not do that.

Fictional business case

Put the ideas to work

A fictional concept called ListingLift would help independent retailers turn a short product fact sheet into a reviewed online listing. The team has a three-slide storyboard and two sample listings, but no application. A reviewer likes the layout yet cannot tell who checks product claims before publication. For a complete supplied-output exercise, use this fictional product fact sheet: Cedar ceramic mug, 12-ounce capacity, $20, dishwasher-safe, local pickup. The flawed sample listing says: “Handmade from 100% recycled materials, this unbreakable mug includes free delivery.” Evaluate the additions against the fact sheet and produce a reviewed listing or annotated correction.

Discuss the decision

  1. What is the product’s core user benefit?
  2. What should the storyboard show about review and approval?
  3. What can the current artifact demonstrate, and which claims would be premature?

Read the numbers before deciding

  1. What is the correct-interpretation rate for each storyboard?
  2. Express the change in percentage points and as a relative change.
  3. What can and cannot be concluded from five repeated classroom reviews?
Compare your reasoning with the worked answer

The first rate is 2 ÷ 5 = 40%; the revised rate is 4 ÷ 5 = 80%. The increase is 40 percentage points, or 100% relative to the original 40% rate. One reviewer represents 20 percentage points in a group of five, so the percentage is sensitive to a single response. The exercise suggests a clearer approval step for these fictional reviewers; it does not establish customer demand, willingness to pay, or causal proof that the revision alone produced the difference. Record the counts, not only the impressive-looking relative increase.

  1. 1Choose one fictional product or service idea connected to an earlier case brief.
  2. 2Write a value proposition naming the user, problem, and useful result.
  3. 3Implement a small usable document product, reporting kit, form/template process, or reviewed service sample. An interface mockup must connect to a usable manual output or process, not stand alone.
  4. 4Identify where AI assistance and human judgment would contribute.

Discussion · make your reasoning visible

Make the Product Idea Understandable

Describe a digital product or AI-enabled service concept and the tangible artifact you would use to explain it. Identify one question that artifact can answer and one business claim it cannot yet support. Write 150–250 words and respond to one classmate in 75–100 words with a specific question or improvement. Distinguish case facts, assumptions, and AI-generated suggestions; disclose any AI assistance. Use fictional or supplied case data. Use the instructor’s calendar for your live offering.

Suggested semester scoring: 5 points, with 3 for the original contribution and 2 for a constructive reply. Follow the arrangements designated for your offering. Independent learners can write a response and compare it with a peer.

What is enough to test an early digital-product concept in this course?
A concept artifact should make the proposed experience tangible enough to discuss or test. It need not be functioning software, but it must show how a specific user’s problem would be addressed.
