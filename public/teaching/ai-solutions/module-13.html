Seminar · understand the decision

Ideas to work with

Lead with the decision

An executive audience needs to know what decision you recommend and why it matters. Open with the business problem, your recommendation, and the expected value. Then provide the evidence necessary to assess it. A chronological account of every activity is usually less useful than a clear argument. Explain uncertainty directly rather than burying it in an appendix. A customer-facing pitch and an executive recommendation use different emphases. A customer needs the useful outcome, scope, price, trust, and next action. An internal sponsor needs value, resources, risk, and an approval decision. Your final presentation may address either route while making the whole business logic assessable.

Make slides serve the argument

Use six to eight slides with a clear purpose for each: problem and customer, proposed experience, evidence, business model, economics, risks/adoption, and recommendation. A storyboard or sample output can make the concept concrete. Keep labels readable and distinguish illustrative material from observed results. A software demonstration is not required; the audience should understand the business proposition through the artifact.

Prepare for constructive questions

Questions reveal whether your reasoning is complete. Be ready to explain why you chose this option, what evidence supports it, what could go wrong, and what would change your mind. When you do not know, identify the missing information and how you would obtain it. Confident delivery does not require pretending uncertainty has been resolved.

Worked opening and decision slide

A useful opening is: “I recommend a limited comparison of reviewed AI-assisted product listings with our current template process. The business question is whether total preparation effort can fall without unsupported product claims. The main uncertainty is review time, so I am asking for a bounded learning exercise rather than a full launch.” A closing decision slide can name the owner, 20 fictional comparison cases, measures, stop conditions, and a decision date. If both methods are applied to all 20 cases, the earlier 12-minute manual and eight-minute assisted assumptions imply about 6.67 staff hours before planning and analysis; do not promise the pilot can be completed in one hour. A planning allowance of roughly eight staff hours would itself remain an estimate. Keep the hypothetical business request distinct from student course requirements: no paid purchase or external public deployment is required; the capstone still includes its agreed controlled classroom or partner deployment.

Fictional business case

Put the ideas to work

A student opens a presentation with five minutes of tool features and finishes by asking for a $2,000 pilot. The audience still does not know the customer problem, the pilot’s success measure, or who will review outputs. The $2,000 figure is the student’s unsupported request, not an established budget or vendor quote. The class should identify what scope, cost basis, responsibility, and decision rule would be needed before the audience could assess it.

  2. Which slides would make the investment decision assessable?
  3. How could the student answer a question about an unknown cost responsibly?

Read the numbers before deciding

  1. What chart would show the time comparison clearly?
  2. What title or caption would prevent the chart from implying observed cash savings?
  3. Which accompanying number or limitation must remain visible?
Compare your reasoning with the worked answer

A two-bar chart can compare estimated monthly staff hours, using a zero baseline and a clearly labeled hours axis. A suitable title is “Estimated preparation time under base assumptions,” with a note that figures are fictional and include review. The decrease is 6.67 hours, about 33.3% of 20 hours. Show the $60 monthly tool expense separately and state that payroll reduction and additional customer value have not been established. A truncated axis or a title such as “Proven savings” would overstate the evidence. The figure should make a specific decision easier to assess, not merely decorate the deck.

Workshop · create an artifact

Rehearse the executive recommendation

  1. 1Draft six to eight slides using the proposal and economics table. Include the usable artifact, route to users/customers, offer or approval request, and delivery/improvement plan.
  2. 2Include the implemented artifact and an early deployment/user-test record. State where it was made available, who tested it by role, what task occurred, and what changed.
  3. 3Practice a seven-minute presentation that ends with a specific decision request.
  4. 4Use instructor/peer feedback or self-review to identify two revisions. Check that the requested pilot scope, time allowance, measures, and responsibilities fit together.
  5. 5Prepare answers to three likely questions about evidence, economics, or risk.
  6. 6Use this module’s data table to calculate and interpret the requested quantities. Keep counts, denominators, units, and uncertainty visible; incorporate the relevant table or small figure into the existing artifact rather than creating another submission.

Discussion · make your reasoning visible

Practice Pitch and Useful Feedback

Share a concise version of your recommendation, its strongest evidence, and one unresolved concern. In your peer response, identify one question a business decision-maker would ask and suggest a specific improvement to the argument. Write 150–250 words and respond to one classmate in 75–100 words with a specific question or improvement. Distinguish case facts, assumptions, and AI-generated suggestions; disclose any AI assistance. Use fictional or supplied case data. Use the instructor’s calendar for your live offering.

Suggested semester scoring: 5 points, with 3 for the original contribution and 2 for a constructive reply. Follow the arrangements designated for your offering. Independent learners can write a response and compare it with a peer.
