Learn about a recent event before proposing a tool

“Would you like an AI assistant?” invites a reaction to your idea. “Tell me about the last booking question that took longer than expected” invites a description of work. Follow with what happened, what the person tried, who helped, and what the delay cost. Ask about a contrary example as well. Your aim is to learn what should change, not collect compliments.

Cedar Desk’s coordinator wants easier policy checking. The customer wants an unambiguous next step. The owner wants control over commitments and expense. Interviewing one role does not establish the needs or purchasing authority of the others. The owner’s willingness to consider a test is not a purchase order.

A typical-customer question favors the median; a total-burden question needs the total. Neither summary alone describes every experience. These are customer waits, not minutes of coordinator labor.

Six of eight records describe repeated delay: 75%. Three of eight say they would try a pilot: 37.5%. Those percentages summarize different questions. They cannot be multiplied into a forecast of paying customers. With eight convenience records, one person changes a percentage by 12.5 percentage points.

Investigate before proposing the solution

Use the eight supplied waits and discovery statements to choose a claim you can defend. Predict what one longer wait would change, explore the records, and commit to a recommendation before opening the challenge. Afterward, retain or revise your recommendation and explain the evidence behind it.

For a short discovery role-play, a partner can play the coordinator or owner from the case card and answer “unknown” when a fact is missing. Ask about a recent event, the current workaround, a contrary example, and what they would need to observe before approving a test. Use the worked explanation below if a respondent is unavailable.

Use the conversation to sharpen the problem

The supplied coordinator response is: “I know many answers already. Finding the latest policy and asking about exceptions takes time.” This suggests a source-access and handoff problem; it does not establish that drafting is the bottleneck. The customer's concern about an unconfirmed change suggests clarity matters alongside speed. The two records without repeated delay challenge a claim that every interaction needs intervention.

Ask: “Tell me about the last customer reply that was delayed. What happened, and what did you do?” Follow up on frequency, consequences, and the current alternative. Avoid questions that prescribe an amazing AI tool or ask for a hypothetical purchase promise. Six of eight reporting need and three of eight willing to try are separate findings; their overlap is not supplied, and neither count is payment evidence.

If a respondent is unavailable, label this “analysis of fictional case responses.” Do not claim you conducted the conversation. A real classroom role-play is still not a real customer interview. Eight convenience observations do not establish representative market demand.

Carry forward: write one neutral question, a baseline with its denominator and period, one contrary observation, and an uncertainty that would change the problem statement. Keep your initial and revised interpretation so the reason for the change stays visible.
