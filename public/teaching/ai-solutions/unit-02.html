Discover the need

Who experiences the problem, how often, and what happens today?

Learn

By the end of this unit

  • Ask neutral questions and separate need evidence from interest in a solution.
  • Choose and explain a numerical summary using the correct denominator.

Planned guided work including textbook reading: 83–133 minutes, plus project work. This is a design estimate.

Learn about a recent event before proposing a tool

“Would you like an AI assistant?” invites a reaction to your idea. “Tell me about the last booking question that took longer than expected” invites a description of work. Follow with what happened, what the person tried, who helped, and what the delay cost. Ask about a contrary example as well. Your aim is to learn what should change, not collect compliments.

Cedar Desk’s coordinator wants easier policy checking. The customer wants an unambiguous next step. The owner wants control over commitments and expense. Interviewing one role does not establish the needs or authority of the others. The owner’s willingness to consider a test is not approval for live use.

The total is 72 minutes. The mean is 72 ÷ 8 = 9 minutes. The ordered middle values are 6 and 6, so the median is 6. The 31-minute wait matters even though it does not change the median much. A typical-customer question favors the median; a total-burden question needs the total. Neither summary alone describes every experience. These are customer waits, not minutes of coordinator labor.

Six of eight records describe repeated delay: 75%. Three of eight say they would try a pilot: 37.5%. Those percentages summarize different questions. They cannot be multiplied into a forecast of paying customers. With eight convenience records, one person changes a percentage by 12.5 percentage points.

2.5: Measures of the Center of the Data

Barbara Illowsky and Susan Dean. Introductory Statistics 2e. 2nd edition. OpenStax, Rice University, 2023.

Read: Read the first two paragraphs defining mean, median, and outliers. Then jump to Example 2.27 and read its problem and explanation; its displayed arithmetic can be checked with an ordinary calculator. Skip other examples, formulas, and calculator instructions.

Why this unit: Shows why an average can hide an unusual value and why a typical result should be described carefully.

Course connection: For any discipline, translate the book's customer into the intended user and its opportunity into a worthwhile problem. This is an application of the research principle; a friendly response is not proof of demand.

Course connection: Report the baseline, revised result, sample size, and variation before making an impact claim. Time saved is potential capacity; it becomes a cash saving only when an actual expense is reduced. Include review time, rework, setup, and ongoing tool costs in the course's original resource table. These readings support interpreting results, not a guarantee of financial return.

Use the conversation to sharpen the problem

The supplied coordinator response is: “I know many answers already. Finding the latest policy and asking about exceptions takes time.” This suggests a source-access and handoff problem; it does not establish that drafting is the bottleneck. The customer's concern about an unconfirmed change suggests clarity matters alongside speed. The two records without repeated delay challenge a claim that every interaction needs intervention.

Ask: “Tell me about the last customer reply that was delayed. What happened, and what did you do?” Follow up on frequency, consequences, and the current alternative. Avoid questions that prescribe an amazing AI tool or ask for a hypothetical purchase promise. Six of eight reporting need and three of eight willing to try are separate findings; their overlap is not supplied, and neither count is payment evidence.

If a respondent is unavailable, label this “analysis of fictional case responses.” Do not claim you conducted the conversation. A real classroom role-play is still not a real customer interview. Eight convenience observations do not establish representative market demand.

Carry forward: write one neutral question, a baseline with its denominator and period, one contrary observation, and an uncertainty that would change the problem statement. Keep your initial and revised interpretation so the reason for the change stays visible.

Compare with the worked reasoning

The observed story supports investigating information-finding friction, not demand for a broad AI product. Ask what they checked, where they got stuck, and what resolved the task. One fictional story does not establish frequency across users, willingness to pay, or adoption.
