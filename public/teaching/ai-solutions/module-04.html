Seminar · understand the decision

Ideas to work with

Design the customer experience, not only the reply

A service encounter includes what happens before, during, and after a response. Ask how the customer enters the process, what information they need, how they know the issue is resolved, and what happens if the response is wrong. AI may help draft or organize a response, while a person remains responsible for exceptions. Convenience for the business should not create more work for the customer. Delivery begins after the customer or internal sponsor accepts the offer. Define intake, preparation, approval, handoff, support, and follow-up. A useful deliverable should meet the promised scope and tell the recipient what to do next.

Define boundaries and escalation

Some questions can be answered from a clear policy; others require judgment, authorization, or information that is unavailable. A service concept should state what the assistant may answer, what it must not promise, and when a staff member takes over. A handoff is useful only if the customer knows what will happen next and the staff member receives enough context to help.

Balance speed with quality

Faster replies are not automatically better service. A useful evaluation includes policy accuracy, clear language, resolution, customer effort, and appropriate escalation. Consider both routine and exception cases. A storyboard or sample conversation can reveal problems before the business invests in a system. In this course, evaluating those artifacts is sufficient; a working chatbot is not required. Count missed exceptions separately from unnecessary escalations. The first uses all true exceptions as its denominator; the second uses all routine requests. Overall accuracy can look high when most requests are easy, even if a consequential minority is handled poorly. Plain-language error counts make the business tradeoff clearer than a single score. To study retention, define an eligible cohort and time window: for example, buyers with a full month in which to reorder. Repeat-purchase rate is repeat buyers divided by those eligible buyers, not repeat orders divided by every historical lead. For internal services, continued use and task completion may be more relevant than purchases.

Worked artifact: a useful handoff, not a false confirmation

An appropriate response is: “Regular check-in begins after 3 p.m. Early check-in depends on staff confirmation, so I cannot guarantee access at 9 a.m. You can ask the front desk to confirm whether an earlier room is available.” In a proposed service storyboard, the next scene can show a request routed to the front desk with the requested arrival time, the policy question, and the fact that no promise has been made. Label that scene as a proposed process; do not say a real request was sent. The final scene shows staff confirming availability or explaining the available choices. A quality review asks whether the guest understands the uncertainty, knows the next step, and receives a consistent answer. Do not invent luggage storage, compensation, or a response-time commitment absent from the policy.

Concept studio · explore before the case

Route the request, not just the words

Choose a customer request, then decide what the service should do. The best route depends on information and authority, not on whether an AI can compose an answer.

Original teaching example · all case facts and observations are fictional.

Carry this into your decision

A useful service design specifies the supported answer, missing-information path, escalation owner, and customer expectation. A handoff is part of the product.

Fictional business case

Put the ideas to work

A fictional hotel, Harbor Stay, permits check-in after 3 p.m. Early check-in depends on staff confirmation. A guest asks for guaranteed 9 a.m. access because of a morning flight. A draft AI reply confirms the request without checking availability. The front desk is the only role authorized in this fictional case to confirm room availability. No luggage-storage policy, compensation policy, or guaranteed response time is supplied. The storyboard may propose a handoff, but it must not imply that an actual booking or request has been made.

Discuss the decision

  1. What is the customer trying to accomplish beyond receiving an answer?
  2. Where should the service concept require staff involvement?
  3. How would you judge a response that is slower but prevents a false promise?

Business numbers · interpret the evidence

Service accuracy can hide the costly mistakes

Fictional teaching data, not real customer, vendor, or pilot observations. A simulated routing exercise for 80 requests. Read the numbers before deciding

  1. What percentage of all requests was routed correctly?
  2. What percentage of true exceptions was missed, and what percentage of routine requests was escalated unnecessarily?
  3. Why is the overall accuracy percentage insufficient for a service decision?
Compare your reasoning with the worked answer

Correct routing is 54 routine requests plus 16 escalated exceptions: 70 ÷ 80 = 87.5%. Missed exceptions are 4 ÷ 20 = 20% of true exceptions. Unnecessary escalations are 6 ÷ 60 = 10% of routine requests. These are different denominators. A missed exception can produce a false promise or unresolved customer need; an unnecessary escalation adds staff work and delay. Both matter, but their consequences need not be equal. Calling all 58 routine-service cases “successfully automated” would conceal the four missed exceptions. Show the two error types alongside the headline rate.

Discussion · make your reasoning visible

When Should a Person Take Over?

Describe a fictional customer-service situation where AI assistance could help. Identify a clear escalation trigger, explain the customer’s next step, and show how you would evaluate the quality of the handoff. Write 150–250 words and respond to one classmate in 75–100 words with a specific question or improvement. Distinguish case facts, assumptions, and AI-generated suggestions; disclose any AI assistance. Use fictional or supplied case data. Use the instructor’s calendar for your live offering.

Suggested semester scoring: 5 points, with 3 for the original contribution and 2 for a constructive reply. Follow the arrangements designated for your offering. Independent learners can write a response and compare it with a peer.
