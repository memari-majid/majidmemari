An adaptable partner challenge

Each offering centers on a business need and the resources actually available that semester. Students discover and scope the problem, design an AI-assisted solution, implement a usable product or service with existing no-code tools, deploy it in a controlled pilot, test it with a user, and pitch a supported adoption or sales recommendation. Finance, Marketing, MBA/strategy, MIS, and Operations perspectives contribute to the same journey. The core lessons, outcomes, milestones, and rubrics remain stable; the partner brief, data, tools, deliverable, and pilot scope can change.

Memari will provide the semester’s confirmed partner or classroom-client brief, available resources, and delivery arrangements before project selection. Published example cases are practice material; an instructor-led classroom client offers the same milestones when external access is unavailable.

Examples in this course illustrate the method. They do not require every semester to build the same product or start the same business.

Start with the project brief

The instructor and problem owner establish these conditions before students commit to a solution.

Problem and decision
What recurring problem, need, or issue does the partner want addressed, who experiences it, and what decision would a useful solution support?
User and sponsor
Who will use the artifact, who owns the problem, and who can approve the pilot scope and any real-world use?
Available evidence and data
Which public, supplied, synthetic, or appropriately approved data can students actually use? What must remain unknown or excluded?
Tools and budget
Which existing AI, document, spreadsheet, form, presentation, or other no-code tools are available without requiring students to purchase access?
Time and skills
What can be implemented, tested, and handed off within the semester and students’ business skills? Reduce scope before adding technical prerequisites.
Partner availability
Can the partner provide a brief, scope/readiness feedback, a pilot user, and a final response? Who substitutes if a checkpoint is missed?
Deployment and maintenance
Where can a controlled version be made available to a test user, and who owns access, support, revision, and the decision to continue or stop?
What belongs in the partner problem brief?
  • Business context and specific problem statement
  • User, problem owner, sponsor, and test-user role
  • Current process or alternative and a credible baseline
  • Desired outcome and numerical quality/value measures
  • Available data and explicit unknowns or exclusions
  • Available tools, budget, student skills, and time constraints
  • Agreed minimum usable deliverable and pilot location
  • Acceptance checks, stop conditions, and escalation owner
  • Partner checkpoint availability and classroom fallback
  • Ownership, handoff, support, and what happens after the course

What counts as implementation and deployment?

  • Deploy a minimum usable solution that a test user can access and use for the agreed task. Examples include a shared document product, spreadsheet-supported briefing, form-and-template process, reviewed content/customer-service kit, or a manually operated AI-assisted service.
  • A private classroom delivery is valid: provide a dated/versioned file or approved shared location, verify the recipient can open it, and observe or record a defined user task. A public website, app store, payment system, custom code, or live integration is not required.
  • A standalone pitch deck, attractive mockup, or plan is not sufficient for the final capstone. A prototype interface must connect to a usable manual deliverable or service process that can be tested end to end.
  • Keep a short deployment and test record: version, location/delivery method, user role, task, access check, actual result, relevant quality/time measures, feedback, revision, and remaining limits. Fictional inputs are allowed; do not invent the user-test outcome.
  • Obtain the partner’s agreement before external use of its workflow, data, brand, or customer-facing material; use the instructor-approved classroom route if approval or access is unavailable.
  • Use human review for final claims, decisions, and exceptions. Define a stop/fallback step and a handoff owner. An AI-agent role is a bounded delegated task, not permission for unsupported autonomous actions.

An equivalent classroom route

If no partner is available, access is delayed, or the scope exceeds available resources, Memari supplies a fictional or public-information brief and acts as sponsor. A classmate or instructor serves as the test user. Students still implement a usable artifact, make it available in a private classroom setting, run a real classroom user task with supplied inputs, and document the outcome. This route uses the same rubric and deadlines; it is not a lower-grade option and does not require external outreach.

Evaluate, pitch, and hand off

Evidence for the final decision

  • Grade problem fit, implementation usefulness, numerical reasoning, source/evidence quality, testing and revision, responsible AI use, operating feasibility, and communication through the existing rubrics.
  • Assess whether the agreed scope was implemented and tested, not whether it uses the most advanced tool or the largest budget.
  • Partner enthusiasm, actual sales revenue, funding, customer count, and public launch are not grading criteria. A well-supported revise or stop recommendation can earn full credit after a usable solution and controlled test have been completed.
  • Keep the five short briefs as practice that feeds the capstone. The portfolio, readiness plan, proposal, report, and presentation are the existing milestone submissions; retain their points, lengths, and course deadlines.
  • Real partner feedback and classroom-proxy feedback have different evidence limits; label them accurately and judge the quality of interpretation.

A handoff someone can use

  • Final artifact or service kit, version, and agreed access/delivery location
  • Short user instructions and a worked example using permitted information
  • Source/assumption log and review/acceptance checklist
  • Pilot task, actual results, feedback, revisions, and known limitations
  • Named owner for use, corrections, maintenance, and escalation
  • Clear continue/revise/stop recommendation and any unfinished decisions
  • Access/support closeout: what remains available, what is withdrawn, and who is responsible after the course
