Unit 12 of 12

Demonstrate and defend

What should happen next, given the evidence?

Learn

By the end of this unit

  • Demonstrate the actual task and connect the recommendation to traceable evidence.
  • Defend a calculation, a design decision, and a limitation without overstating results.

Make a decision easy to inspect

The final audience needs to understand what the solution does, what you learned, what it costs, and what should happen next. Lead with a proportionate recommendation. Show the task, sources, and review point. Then use the strongest evidence to explain the recommendation rather than narrating every tool you tried.

A concise decision brief can use six sections: need and alternatives; implemented solution; actual test and revision; economics and capacity; route to users and operating plan; recommendation and handoff. Keep the artifact, detailed records, and calculations in supporting files. Reuse corrected earlier work and remove claims that later evidence contradicted.

A figure should make the comparison clearer. The supplied 96-to 84-minute benchmark is a 12.5% reduction. A bar chart beginning at 80 can exaggerate the visual gap; label any restricted axis and prefer a zero baseline for these magnitude bars. Keep the table available, identify the twelve supplied cases, and show quality alongside time. Your own pilot figure must use your actual observations.

Model opening: evidence-supported caution

“I recommend a bounded next test of the reviewed ReplyKit, not a broad rollout yet. The supplied benchmark shows twelve acceptable replies with less complete effort than the manual comparison. That is classroom case evidence, not our own deployment or market proof. The monthly base scenario has zero net capacity value and negative cash change. Our next decision depends on observed use, review effort, and quality.”

Adapt the structure to your actual project. Replace supplied evidence with your observed record where available, keep the distinction visible, and do not copy an event you did not perform.

Try

Rehearse the decision, then change one assumption

Give a short demonstration: locate the source, enter an ordinary or exception question, produce the useful output, show the human check, and locate the record. A video or screenshots can support access needs, but the usable artifact and actual task evidence must still be available.

Ask a reviewer to challenge one assumption: “What if adoption is 30%?”, “What if review adds six minutes?”, or “What if the buyer prefers the existing template?” Answer with the relevant calculation or evidence limit. A clear “we have not established that” is more defensible than an invented answer.

Worked changed-assumption answer

For the supplied monthly scenario,30% of 1,200 is 360 assisted replies. One minute each releases six hours, valued at $144. After $240 recurring expense, net capacity value is − $96. With unchanged payroll, recurring cash change remains − $240. This weakens the value case; it does not prove that every possible use is worthless.

Check access to the handoff files and state what happens after the course: permitted continued use, owner, support boundary, unresolved issues, and closeout. A well-supported decision to narrow or stop can earn strong evaluation when the implementation, test, revision, and reasoning are complete. What will you carry forward?

Your classroom test succeeds, but demand and review cost remain uncertain. Which final recommendation best matches the evidence?

Evidence and assessment criteria

Demonstrate the actual bounded task and defend the recommendation using the artifact, test/retest evidence, economics, and operating handoff. Provide a concise decision brief and presentation or an accessible equivalent using the offering’s announced arrangements.

This milestone’s internal rubric allocates 60% to the shared demonstration/handoff and 40% to individual mastery. In the full-course model, those contribute 15 and 10 percentage points respectively. The individual evidence comes from the checks near Milestones 3 and 5; do not grade it twice. Every learner explains a calculation, a decision, and a limitation. Self-paced completion does not imply instructor grading or a credential.
