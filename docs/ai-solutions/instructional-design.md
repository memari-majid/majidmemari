# Building AI Solutions — instructional design plan

**Owner and sole instructor:** Majid Memari

**Date:** November 11, 2026

**Status:** The owner authorized development on November 10, 2026. The twelve-unit course and visual-learning upgrade have been implemented and released. Technical evidence is recorded in [DEVELOPMENT_AND_VALIDATION.md](development-and-validation.md); representative-learner testing is still required.

This is the current planning document. It takes precedence over the proposed learning sequence and interface direction in the earlier `COURSE_PLAN.md` and `COURSE_DESIGN_AND_DEVELOPMENT.md`. It does not change a live offering's grades, deadlines, enrollment, or submission arrangements. The actual website and local implementation are evidence to evaluate against this blueprint. Archived implementations do not define current requirements.

## 1. The central design decision

**One project, one learning sequence, five assessed milestones.**

The course should help a learner from any discipline take a worthwhile real-world problem through a usable AI-assisted solution, an honest test, a case for adoption, delivery, and a credible recommendation. It should feel like guided progress on that project. Learners should not have to choose between a course, pathways, a simulation suite, a venture studio, and a partner challenge.

**Working title:** Building AI Solutions

**Audience framing:** Practical AI problem-solving for everyone. Students across campus can participate with no prerequisites.

**Promise:** Find a real need. Build a useful solution. Test it. Bring it to users.

Personal, community, research, organizational, and business projects can use the same method. A solo business supported by AI agents is a motivating scenario, not a prediction that every learner must become an entrepreneur. The core includes finding users, making an offer, selling or securing adoption, delivering, and operating responsibly.

The course belongs to Majid Memari and can be offered across institutions and organizations. Its public identity does not contain an institution or course number. Students from any discipline follow the same sequence. Their project can address a need in their field, community, organization, or an emerging business; business-school enrollment and entrepreneurial intent are not assumed.

## 2. Diagnosis of the existing design

These findings come from the current course source, assessment specifications, design documents, user feedback, and observed reference activities. They are a design audit, not results from a learner study.

| Existing issue | Consequence for learning | Proposed correction |
|---|---|---|
| Several competing destinations and calls to action | A novice cannot readily tell what to do first, what is required, or what counts as finished. | One Start/Continue action and one ordered course map. Supporting resources appear at the point of use. |
| Structured discovery is taught in current Module 11, after building in Module 6 and project selection in Module 7 | Students may build before learning how to establish the need and choose scope. | Teach discovery in Unit 2 and select provisional scope in Unit 3. Continue discovery throughout. |
| Five case briefs precede selection of the continuing opportunity | Breadth can become five disconnected mini-projects and repeated writing. | Choose one problem early. Use short examples from other disciplines to teach transfer, without five separate project papers. |
| Ten assessment specifications plus sixteen discussions | Administrative and writing load can crowd out implementation, testing, and feedback. | Five scored revisions of one project record; embedded practice and peer critique are normally formative. |
| The final usable solution is explicit, but early assessment rubrics still resemble generic briefs | Students can infer that writing about a solution matters more than making it usable. | Publish observable implementation, user-task, revision, and handoff criteria at the beginning. |
| Six full labs and ten concept studios are promoted as features | More interaction does not necessarily mean more learning; they can become a second curriculum. | Select each activity because it teaches a particular decision. Integrate it in the lesson and replace overlapping practice. |
| TA is presented as a separate destination | Students leave the lesson and lose context; its role is unclear. | A course-specific TA beside the lesson, with the current lesson identified and course-source links. |
| Design documents mix stable course design with a historical offering | Old points, dates, terminology, and constraints can govern the independent product accidentally. | Keep a stable instructional core and a separate offering configuration. |

The earlier assessment specifications imply approximately **17–27 written pages** across five briefs, portfolio, pilot plan, proposal, and report, before discussions and slides. This is an estimate from the stated page ranges, not a measured student workload. In the old weighting model, the five briefs and opportunity portfolio account for **37%** of the grade: 25% + 20% × 150/250. Neither arrangement should automatically carry into the new design.

Retain the strongest existing work: original business cases, source checking, quantitative examples, honest treatment of uncertainty, accessible tables, usable-artifact requirements, workbook portability, and a full classroom fallback.

## 3. Educational approach

Use backward design: specify the performance students should achieve, determine evidence that would demonstrate it, then select lessons and activities. This prevents a book or a collection of widgets from becoming the curriculum. This ordering follows the [Understanding by Design framework](https://ascd.org/ASCD/pdf/siteASCD/publications/UbD_WhitePaper0312.pdf).

Within lessons, alternate a worked example with a learner decision; revisit important ideas later; pair graphics with nearby explanations; and ask learners to explain their reasoning. These choices are informed by the [IES practice guide on instruction and study](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/1). They are design principles, not evidence that this particular course has already improved learning.

Keep the project authentic and sustained, with critique, revision, and a demonstration to a meaningful audience. The [PBLWorks project-design model](https://www.pblworks.org/gold-standard/pbl-project-design) informs this structure. A classroom client is a valid audience; the course does not require public disclosure or a commercial launch.

Use accessible representations and graduated support while preserving a common destination. A single recommended sequence can still allow learners to revisit explanations, choose an accessible response format, and use a relevant real-world context. This is consistent with the [CAST UDL Guidelines](https://udlguidelines.cast.org/).

**Design rule:** an interaction earns its place only if it helps a learner explain, decide, build, evaluate, or transfer something required by an outcome.

## 4. Learners, prerequisites, and boundaries

There are **no prerequisites**. Assume students from any discipline with uneven AI experience, quantitative confidence, and familiarity with documents or presentations. Do not assume programming, prior AI or business knowledge, statistics software, API configuration, advanced mathematics, experience with a particular tool, or paid AI access. Explain necessary vocabulary, basic calculations, and tool steps at the point of use, with worked examples and accessible support. The supplied-output route must allow participation when an AI account or paid feature is unavailable.

Begin with a short ungraded diagnostic: distinguish a fact from an unsupported AI claim; interpret a simple rate with support; explain a real-world problem without naming a tool. Use the result to offer brief support within the lesson, not a separate track or admission barrier.

Capabilities developed during the course include:

- Understanding generative AI, prediction/classification, workflows, and agents at the level needed to choose and evaluate a use.
- Briefing an existing AI tool, supplying allowed sources, checking output, revising, and documenting human responsibility.
- Producing a usable document kit, decision aid, repeatable AI-assisted service, or no-code experience.
- Interpreting data, error consequences, economics, adoption, and customer evidence.

Algorithm implementation, model training, infrastructure setup, autonomous external actions, public software deployment, and real payment collection are outside the required work. Advanced model mechanics may be optional reference material when they serve an instructor's chosen offering. They must not become hidden prerequisites.

## 5. End outcomes and evidence

| Outcome | Evidence students must be able to produce | What sufficient work demonstrates |
|---|---|---|
| O1. Establish a worthwhile need | Problem statement, user and buyer/sponsor, current alternative, evidence log, baseline | Important claims have an identified source or are labeled assumptions; consequence and scope are specific. |
| O2. Select and design an appropriate AI response | Comparison of current process, simple non-AI improvement, and AI-assisted option; workflow | The chosen option fits resources; inputs, output, AI role, human check, exception, and fallback are explicit. |
| O3. Build a usable solution | Actual artifact or repeatable service plus instructions and varied test cases | Another person can complete the bounded intended task, with any assistance recorded; essential claims and calculations can be checked. A mock-up alone is insufficient. |
| O4. Evaluate and improve it | Test plan, observed user-task record, relevant baseline/comparison, defects, revision and retest | Measures have units and denominators; preparation, review, and rework are included; results are not overstated. |
| O5. Explain viability | Cost and capacity table, scenario comparison, price or resource request, decision threshold | Distinguishes revenue, contribution, cash costs, and released capacity; considers an unfavorable scenario. |
| O6. Reach users and make a credible offer | Buyer/sponsor definition, one channel, offer, sales/adoption conversation, onboarding | Promise matches evidence; scope, cost, objections, and a realistic next step are clear. |
| O7. Operate and defend the solution | Demonstration, concise recommendation, individual explanation, handoff | Learner explains decisions and limits; ownership, review, updates, support, and stopping conditions are clear. |

Three evidence questions remain separate throughout:

1. **Need:** Is there evidence that the problem matters?
2. **Performance:** Does this solution perform the intended task in the tested setting?
3. **Adoption:** Will someone use, approve, or pay for it?

A successful classroom task does not prove market demand. A positive interview does not prove performance. Generated personas do not count as customer observations. A sound recommendation to narrow, revise, or stop can demonstrate excellent learning.

If the non-AI option is preferable, students still demonstrate and evaluate a bounded AI candidate in a controlled classroom comparison. They may recommend the simpler option for continued operation. They do not have to deploy an inferior AI option to a partner to satisfy the course; the required evidence is competent AI use, comparison, evaluation, and judgment.

## 6. One proposed course sequence

Twelve units are proposed to organize the work. They are **not twelve mandatory calendar weeks**. A semester can reserve additional meetings for building, partner feedback, revision, and demonstrations. A workshop uses a clearly stated subset and does not claim completion of the full course.

| Unit | Central question and core learning | Practice within the lesson | Addition to the same project record |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. AI and a useful business task** | What can AI contribute, and what must a person verify? Identify generative, predictive, workflow, and agent uses through examples. | Compare a supplied AI output with its sources; repair an unsupported claim. See a finished capstone example. | Initial need, intended user, and AI-use/review log. |
| **2. Discover the need** | Who experiences the problem, how often, and what happens today? Distinguish user, buyer, and sponsor; ask neutral questions. | Practice a short neutral discovery conversation with a classmate or classroom client, then revise the evidence log. Inspect supplied notes and a baseline table; compare mean, median, total, and denominator. Supplied responses provide an access fallback. | Evidence log, neutral questions, current process, and initial baseline. |
| **3. Choose and scope a solution** | Which response fits the need, uncertainty, and resources? Compare current practice, simple improvement, and AI assistance. | Make a constrained choice; change one uncertain estimate and explain whether the choice changes. | One user, one task, one output, success measure, quality boundary, and exclusions. **Milestone 1.** |
| **4. Design the workflow** | How does an input become a useful, checked output? Distinguish a fixed workflow from an agent with choices. | Trace a case through intake, approved sources, AI draft, human review, exception, and delivery. | Workflow, AI role brief, source requirements, reviewer, and manual fallback. |
| **5. Build the first usable version** | Can someone use what we made? Use an existing tool to produce a kit, service, or no-code experience. | Follow one complete construction example, then test ordinary, incomplete, and exception inputs. | Usable version, three varied bench tests, defects, and first revision. **Milestone 2.** |
| **6. Design a fair test** | What would count as improvement, unacceptable quality, or an inconclusive result? | Compare policies on the same small dataset. Interpret missed exceptions, unnecessary review, full-process time, and sample limits. | Pilot task, comparison, measures, success/stop rules, observation sheet, and approved scope. |
| **7. Pilot, revise, and retest** | What happened when another person tried it? Observe the task rather than coach the user through every step. | Critique a supplied pilot record before conducting the class/partner task. Interpret before-and-after results without claiming causation automatically. | Actual user-task observation, revision, retest, and stated limitations. **Milestone 3.** |
| **8. Calculate value and capacity** | Does the benefit survive preparation, review, rework, delivery, and support? | Inspect a value bridge and less favorable scenario; distinguish cash savings from capacity value. | Economics and capacity model with units, periods, assumptions, and a decision threshold. |
| **9. Shape the offer** | Who pays or approves, what is included, and why choose this? | Compare two offers and revise an unsupported promise; inspect price, cost, and scope together. | Offer card, price/resource request, alternatives, delivery terms, and support boundary. |
| **10. Find users and sell or secure adoption** | How does a prospect or sponsor reach a justified next step? | Interpret a small acquisition funnel; practice discovery, objection handling, and an honest close. | One channel, message, funnel assumptions, sales/adoption conversation, and onboarding. **Milestone 4.** |
| **11. Make delivery dependable** | Who owns quality, exceptions, updates, and continued use? | Step through an exception and a recovery; compare bounded AI roles with unchecked delegation. | Operating instructions, AI/human responsibility table, maintenance, handoff, and retention/adoption measures. |
| **12. Demonstrate and defend** | What should happen next, given the evidence? | Rehearse the demonstration and answer a changed-assumption question. Revise a misleading figure. | Demonstration, concise decision brief, evidence figure, pitch, individual defense, and final handoff. **Milestone 5.** |

Discovery continues after Unit 2. Rough economics inform scope in Unit 3 before the fuller analysis in Unit 8. Source checks and human responsibility begin in Unit 1. Learners may revise an earlier decision without leaving the common course path.

Different disciplines offer perspectives on the same decisions through a common sequence. For example, a community program, research team, creative practice, or service business may need clear communication, dependable intake, quality control, affordable delivery, and an adoption plan. Short examples demonstrate transfer without imposing an additional project or specialist background. The continuing Cedar Desk business case remains a complete, explained starting point for everyone.

## 7. Capstone and assessment design

### One living project record

Keep a single project folder/dossier: problem and evidence; solution and workflow; tests and revision; economics and offer; delivery and handoff. Each milestone is a snapshot of new evidence and changes. Do not ask learners to rewrite the same background in five papers.

The final synthesis can be a concise 4–6-page decision brief with linked evidence and a 6–8-slide presentation. The usable artifact, observations, calculations, and operating instructions live in supporting files. These are proposed limits to be tested with learners, not existing policies.

| Milestone | At unit | Proposed weight for a new full offering | Main evidence |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| 1. Problem and feasible scope | 3 | 15% | Need, baseline, evidence quality, alternatives, bounded task |
| 2. First usable solution | 5 | 20% | Usability, source checks, AI contribution, bench tests, revision |
| 3. Pilot and learning | 7 | 25% | Test design, observed task, quantitative interpretation, revision/retest |
| 4. Viability and route to users | 10 | 15% | Economics, capacity, offer, channel, selling or adoption, onboarding |
| 5. Demonstration and handoff | 12 | 25% | 15% final demonstration/handoff; 10% individual explanation of evidence and decisions |
| **Total** | | **100%** | One cumulative project; no separate individual-mastery upload |

Weights are proposals for a new course configuration. Existing offering grades are unchanged. Instructor-led teams may share artifacts, but every learner must explain a calculation, a design decision, and a limitation. Individual study uses the same evidence and self-checks; the website must not imply instructor grading or a credential.

Use a short common rubric vocabulary: **problem fit, evidence, usability, evaluation, economics, judgment, communication**. Score the criteria relevant to the milestone. Give feedback on the next consequential improvement.

Example evaluation criterion:

| Level | Observable performance |
|---|---|
| Not yet demonstrated | No actual task record, unverifiable results, or only a claim that the AI works. |
| Developing | Records a task, but comparison, denominator, quality check, or full-process time is missing. |
| Meets the goal | Traceable observations; defensible comparison; quality and complete effort measured; limitations stated; a meaningful revision and retest documented. |
| Strong transfer | Explains an alternative interpretation, evaluates whether the improvement transfers to a changed case, and justifies a proportionate next decision. |

Formative questions, simulation decisions, retrieval practice, and peer critique prepare the milestones. They are not an expanding collection of separately graded submissions. Readiness checks protect the validity of the pilot: if the artifact or test is not ready, revise it before proceeding with an external user. A favorable result is never a prerequisite for a good grade.

## 8. The recurring lesson experience

Student-facing steps: **Learn → Try → Build → Finish.** One primary action advances the current step. Back and a compact outline remain available; the sequence is guidance, not forced clicking.

- **Learn:** one concrete decision, one or two observable outcomes, a short explanation, and a worked example. Introduce terms beside the example.
- **Try:** make a prediction, change a meaningful decision or inspect evidence, then see a linked figure/table and an explanation. Feedback responds to the learner's reasoning or misconception.
- **Build:** apply that reasoning to the continuing project and save one small addition or revision. Offer one template and a relevant example.
- **Finish:** answer a short transfer/retrieval question, identify the work just added, and see the next step. A completion marker records study progress, not mastery or submission.

Suggested initial design budget: 15–20 minutes Learn, 15–25 Try, 30–60 Build, and 5–10 Finish. These **65–115 minutes per unit** are planning estimates for the guided lesson, not the full course workload. Building, user testing, independent reading, team coordination, and revision require additional time. Pilot the timings before committing to a semester workload or academic-credit claim.

Fade support: early units show a complete example and partially completed template; middle units leave a consequential choice to the learner; late units require independent selection and defense. Revisit source checking, denominators, uncertainty, and full-process cost in later units rather than treating them as one-time topics.

### Worked design example: Unit 6

**Decision:** Which review policy is ready for a small classroom pilot of an AI-assisted customer reply service?

**Outcome:** Given the same supplied cases, compare quality and total effort, explain why speed alone is insufficient, and specify a bounded next test.

**Fictional teaching data, not observed partner results:**

| Policy on the same 12 cases | Acceptable final replies | Material errors | Total preparation, checking, and rework |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Existing manual process | 12/12 | 0 | 96 minutes |
| AI draft with no verification | 9/12 | 3 | 24 minutes |
| AI draft with source check and exception review | 12/12 | 0 | 84 minutes |

Storyboard:

1. Show the task, approved facts, and quality criteria. Ask which policy the learner initially expects to choose and why.
2. Work one ordinary case and one exception. Animate only the movement through draft, check, and escalation; let the learner advance it manually.
3. Reveal one policy at a time on a common scale. Keep the corresponding table visible. Selecting a policy highlights its row and bar together.
4. Ask for a recommendation. “Fastest” feedback points to the three material errors. “No AI can help” feedback points to the tested 12-minute reduction. A justified guarded-pilot choice still receives the limitation: 0/12 errors does not establish safety on future cases.
5. Calculate 96 − 84 = 12 minutes, a 12.5% reduction for this supplied case set. At an explicitly assumed $24/hour, that is $4.80 of released capacity value; unchanged payroll means no demonstrated cash saving. Tool costs remain a separate missing input.
6. Change one estimate: six additional minutes of rework makes the assisted total 90 minutes and the reduction 6.25%. Ask whether the original decision still holds. These are hypothetical sensitivity values, not new observed data.
7. Transfer: the learner defines one task, one quality boundary, full-process timing, and a stop rule for the actual capstone. Save this in the pilot plan.
8. Finish with a new case: a higher average score hides one serious exception. Ask the learner to explain the next action in two sentences.

The lesson has one consequential experiment. It does not require a second full course on classification or model fitting. Its answer key must show the calculation, the evidence boundary, and what would change the recommendation.

## 9. Simulations, figures, animation, and meaningful color

The [reference audit](reference-analysis.md) records observations and limits. The useful patterns are a concrete decision, small inspectable data, staged reasoning, linked views, constrained choices, and feedback. Reproduce these learning functions in original cases rather than copying the reference's wording, artwork, code, or technical syllabus.

**Three substantial embedded experiences are sufficient for the initial core:**

| Experience | Position | Required reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Evidence and business numbers | Units 2 and 6, as short connected exercises | Counts versus rates; distribution and total effort; small-sample limits |
| Quality, review, and exceptions | Units 4–6 | Inputs, human handoff, error consequences, a workable review policy |
| Pilot value and adoption | Units 7–8 | Full-process effort, actual use, capacity, recurring cost, and assumptions |

Marketing, pricing, and opportunity selection still receive worked figures and short choices in the core. Deeper existing marketing/product/portfolio simulations can be optional expansions **inside the relevant lesson**, not a second suite or required track. The draft 24-concept lab expansion is a content pool, not a commitment to require 24 scenes.

Every substantial activity needs a written specification before implementation: outcome; misconception; fixed facts; student decisions; uncertain estimates; data provenance; calculation/model; accessible figure/table; expected feedback; debrief; capstone contribution; approximate time; reset behavior; and a noninteractive alternative.

Visual rules:

- Each chart answers one question and labels units, denominator, period, and comparison. Use a stable scale for comparisons; disclose a changed scale.
- Bind a highlighted chart element to its table row and explanation. Do not animate numbers without a reason to track the change.
- Give each color a stable local meaning and a visible legend. Use navy for structural information, blue for the learner's selected option, teal for a reference or supported result, amber for uncertainty/review, and red for a material error or loss. Category charts use distinct labeled categories; teal does not automatically mean “correct.”
- Pair color with words, symbols, line patterns, or position. Selection, actual outcome, and recommendation must not be encoded by the same unlabelled color.
- Use animation for sequence, accumulation, reassignment, or comparison. Include step, pause/replay, and reduced-motion behavior. No decorative autoplay or attention-grabbing loop.
- Provide text and table equivalents, keyboard controls, meaningful focus, and readable mobile layouts. Do not force precision dragging.
- Formulas and extra detail are available on demand. Core decisions use plain language and ordinary arithmetic.

A simulation's “best” numerical result is not the only acceptable recommendation. Students must consider constraints, uncertainty, and tradeoffs. Do not let them improve results merely by changing an unsupported demand or cost assumption.

## 11. Partner adaptation without changing the learner path

Before an offering, Majid selects one or two bounded partner briefs, or one complete classroom case. Partners contribute a problem and periodic feedback; Majid owns instruction and grading.

Required brief: user and buyer/sponsor; current process; evidence available; acceptable data; desired outcome; resources and approved tools; sharing limits; feedback windows; pilot task; acceptance criteria; and handoff owner. Scope should fit the learner's time and available access.

Use three partner touchpoints: initial brief; scope/pilot review; final demonstration and response. Gather questions centrally. Establish the supplied-case fallback before students depend on an external reply.

A case packet should include approved source facts, sample inputs, baseline data, ordinary and exception cases, a deliberately flawed AI output, an example deliverable, evaluation criteria, and instructions for a classroom reviewer. This lets the same sequence work without special access, confidential data, or a partner able to attend every class.

“Deployment” means the intended test user can access and use the solution in the approved setting. It may be a privately shared kit, a supervised service, or a no-code workflow. State exactly what was delivered and tested; a link, a screenshot, or an intention to launch is not equivalent to a user completing the task.

## 13. Workload and facilitation by one instructor

Proposed planning assumption: 30 learners in ten teams of three. This is not known enrollment. Team size, meeting hours, individual delivery, and academic-credit requirements are offering choices.

The two individual checks are scheduled around Milestones 3 and 5 and together supply the proposed 10% individual component. Each learner explains a calculation, a decision, and an evidence limit across those checks. Final team questions may clarify that evidence; the team presentation is not expected to replace all individual checks.

Five milestone reviews × ten teams × 15 minutes = **12.5 hours** of milestone review across the course. Two five-minute individual checks × 30 learners = **5 hours**. Ten demonstrations at 7 minutes plus 3 minutes questions and 2 minutes transition = **120 minutes**. These estimates exclude preparation, teaching, ordinary coaching, moderation, and resubmissions; test them before promising workload.

Provisional learner-workload budget for the full twelve-unit core:

| Work | Additional hours beyond the guided lesson | Included work |
|---|---:|---|
| Discover and scope, Units 1–3 | 4–6 | Discovery preparation/practice, evidence review, scope revision |
| Design and build, Units 4–5 | 6–10 | Construction beyond the guided Build step, bench tests, correction |
| Test and revise, Units 6–7 | 6–10 | Reviewer coordination, user task, analysis, revision, retest |
| Value and route to users, Units 8–10 | 4–7 | Economics refinement, offer, channel, sales/adoption rehearsal |
| Operation and demonstration, Units 11–12 | 4–7 | Handoff trial, final synthesis, individual preparation, rehearsal |
| **Additional independent work** | **24–40** | Does not repeat time already counted inside Learn/Try/Build/Finish |

Twelve guided lessons at 65–115 minutes contribute **13–23 hours**, giving a provisional core total of **37–63 learner hours**. This is a design estimate, not a claim of academic-credit equivalence or observed effort. If guided work happens in a class meeting, count that time once. Optional reading is not silently required. A formal offering must map its actual contact and independent workload to its own requirements before enrollment; any added practice must serve a stated outcome. Test this estimate with representative learners and inspect whether a project can be completed within the agreed scope.

**Feedback dependency:** propose milestone feedback within five working days and before the next dependent graded decision. Provide a scheduled revision opportunity. Scope feedback must precede substantial building, first-version feedback must precede external piloting, and pilot feedback must precede the final business recommendation. Set actual dates in each offering. When feedback is pending, learners can practice with the supplied case instead of treating silence or a TA response as approval. Self-paced learners use authored examples and rubric checks; no instructor turnaround is implied.

Use one rubric vocabulary, one submission location per offering, brief change logs, a strong and flawed example for each milestone, and a small feedback bank. Give classwide explanations for common misconceptions, then targeted team feedback. Partners do not grade. The TA does not replace instructor assessment.

All formative work must replace or prepare project work. It should not silently add another set of reports, discussions, or platform accounts. Keep the course-wide workload visible and revise estimates after a pilot.

## 15. Design-to-development plan and acceptance gates

The initial planning phase ended when the owner authorized development. The core and its visual upgrade are now released. The sequence below records the design method and remaining validation work; it is not an instruction to stop authorized maintenance. The original plan proposed learner piloting before broad expansion. The core was subsequently developed under owner authorization, and that learner pilot remains outstanding.

1. **Settle the instructional blueprint.** Review the audience, outcomes, twelve-unit sequence, five milestones, scope, and estimated workload. Decide the offering-specific details separately.
2. **Create the assessment package before screens.** One project brief; five milestone specifications; analytic rubric descriptors; an evidence log; and one complete example with a flawed comparison. Trace every outcome to assessed evidence.
3. **Storyboard the units.** For every required activity, specify the decision, misconception, worked example, learner action, feedback, capstone contribution, accessibility alternative, and time budget. No unspecified “add an interactive figure” items.
4. **Complete one representative lesson prototype after planning.** Use the Unit 6 storyboard or equivalent. Include its figure/table, feedback, project task, and course TA in the shared theme. Do not build the entire interaction library first.
5. **Pilot and revise.** Observe 3–5 representative learners from different disciplines, including learners with no prior AI or business knowledge and varied quantitative confidence. This small pilot diagnoses usability and obvious learning problems; it does not establish efficacy.
6. **Revise from the pilot before adding optional depth.** Use learner evidence to improve the existing twelve units. Reconcile any additions against the outcome map and replace redundant work.
7. **Release verification.** Check actual learning flow, source/citation accuracy, numerical results, accessibility, no-TA behavior, progress/resume, final handoff, privacy disclosures, and instructor workload before deployment.

Proposed review gates:

| Gate | Evidence required to pass |
|---|---|
| Alignment | Every outcome has an assessed artifact; each required activity prepares that artifact; no unexplained prerequisites. |
| Coherence | A learner can state the current task, why it matters, what to produce, and what follows. No required side route. |
| Authentic performance | A reviewer can attempt the task using the artifact; test, revision, and handoff are observable. |
| Quantitative integrity | Independent recalculation agrees with displays; units, denominators, assumptions, comparisons, and uncertainty are explicit. |
| Feedback and transfer | Wrong-answer feedback addresses the misconception; a new case tests reasoning beyond memorizing the worked answer. |
| Accessibility and usability | Core work can be completed by keyboard, with reduced motion and text/table alternatives; mobile layout and focus are tested. |
| Assistant and operating reliability | The TA uses the intended course version; sources support claims; course work continues without AI; website inquiry delivery is honestly reported. |
| Feasible workload | Pilot timings and grading effort support the promised offering; activities do not duplicate submissions. |

Initial pilot criteria are design targets, not measured results: learners locate Start without coaching; identify their next action after each step; complete a new decision with an explanation; find feedback and recover from an error; save/resume and locate the finish. Record observed difficulties and revise the design, rather than treating completion clicks or satisfaction ratings as proof of learning.
