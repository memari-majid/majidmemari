## What the course should accomplish

The framing is **Practical AI problem-solving for everyone**. The audience is students from any discipline, with **no prerequisites**. Do not assume programming, prior AI or business knowledge, experience with a specific tool, or paid AI access. Students work with explained examples, numbers, tables, figures, evidence, existing AI tools, and real-world workflows. Check that necessary vocabulary, calculations, and tool steps are taught, with a supplied-output fallback. Students should not have to implement algorithms, configure APIs, or administer infrastructure.

The course takes one project from a real need through a usable AI-assisted solution, evaluation, an impact and resource assessment, preparing for real use, supporting adoption, dependable delivery, and handoff. A partner can supply a need in a field, community, organization, or business; the fictional Cedar Desk case must make the same learning possible without one. Business cases and economics remain useful learning material, without requiring business-school knowledge or a plan to start a business. The partner, tools, budget, and available information can vary by offering.

A usable document kit, repeatable AI-assisted service, decision aid, or no-code experience can qualify. A slide deck, mock-up, or claim that something was built is not sufficient implementation evidence. Learners need an observed user task, revision, retest, and an honest account of limitations. Real revenue, public launch, paid tools, and a favorable business outcome are not prerequisites for learning or assessment success.

Use the current **12 units, one capstone, five milestones, and Learn → Try → Build → Finish** as the review baseline. Preserve one clear path, but recommend combining or shortening units if the evidence shows that this improves learning without losing required outcomes. Simulations belong inside lessons. Students from all disciplines use the common path rather than separate tracks. The course belongs to Majid Memari and must remain reusable across institutions, with no university or course-number branding. The course, personal website, and embedded TA should share a consistent visual theme.

AI-assisted solo businesses are a useful motivating scenario, not a guaranteed prediction. Judge whether students learn to recognize worthwhile demand, delegate bounded work to AI, evaluate results, and remain responsible for consequential decisions.

## Evaluate teaching and assessment

Check whether every important outcome has a taught explanation, a worked example, meaningful practice, feedback, a project application, and observable assessment evidence. Find missing prerequisites, shallow explanations, excessive reading, repeated writing, and places where students can finish by clicking without demonstrating understanding. Judge whether support gradually decreases so students can reason independently by the final milestone.

Check the explicit capability-to-problem map: generation, source-grounded retrieval, extraction/classification, analysis/prediction, images, voice/language, workflows and agents. Every chosen capability needs usable inputs/outputs, a no-code implementation, an appropriate human check and an observed test. Business formation and sales must be optional applications rather than requirements for full credit.

Evaluate the complete path from problem to continued use: need and alternatives, user and responsible decision owner, scope, usable implementation, controlled delivery, evaluation, impact, resource costs and capacity, accessible release instructions, reaching users and supported adoption, onboarding, operations, and handoff. Distinguish learning to implement a solution from merely discussing AI strategy. Check whether ethical judgment, source quality, privacy, human authority, exception handling, and limitations are taught at the point of use.

Assess transfer across disciplines without recommending separate learning paths. Determine whether a student outside a business school can follow the common case and apply its reasoning to a relevant problem. Check that business terminology and quantitative examples are explained rather than treated as prior knowledge. Identify any specialist knowledge or tool access silently assumed by a required task.

Check that milestones reward evidence, usable work, sound reasoning, revision, and individual understanding. A well-supported decision to narrow, revise, or stop should not automatically lose credit. Distinguish personal completion markers from mastery, submitted work, grades, or credentials.

Evaluate one-instructor feasibility: preparation, partner coordination, formative feedback, milestone reviews, individual checks, maintenance, and free-tool access. Treat workload estimates as hypotheses. Identify duplicated submissions or activities that can be removed without losing an outcome. Judge instructor-led and self-paced use separately when support requirements differ.

## Return actionable feedback

Lead with the **three highest-value improvements**, then provide:

1. **Coverage and limits:** pages and steps read, controls and conditions tested, local source version or review date when known, and inaccessible or untested areas. Label findings as observed, inferred, preference, or untested.
2. **Three strengths to preserve:** explain how each supports learning or course operation.
3. **An evidence-based scorecard:** rate curriculum, implementation, end-to-end problem solving, assessment, quantitative reasoning, visual learning, usability/accessibility, transfer across disciplines, TA grounding, readings, and one-instructor feasibility. Use 1 = major gaps, 3 = usable with meaningful improvements, 5 = strong and supported. Mark unverified areas unverified. Do not hide tradeoffs in an average score.
4. **Up to ten prioritized findings:** for each, provide exact URL and section or local file, reproduction steps or evidence, learner consequence, smallest useful improvement, estimated effort with assumptions, and a concrete acceptance check. Use P0 for blocked core learning or materially wrong conclusions, P1 for important changes before teaching, P2 for improvements in the next offering, and P3 for optional polish. Do not invent issues to reach ten.
5. **An alignment map:** connect the major end-to-end outcomes to their unit, practice, project artifact, and milestone evidence. Identify weak or missing links without restating the entire syllabus.
6. **A concrete revision:** rewrite one weak lesson segment or specify one improved activity. Include the outcome, scenario, learner decision, data, feedback, debrief, and project transfer. Explain what existing content it replaces so the course does not become busier.
7. **A simplification and implementation plan:** what to retain, remove, shorten, or combine; what to fix before teaching; what can wait; dependencies and estimated instructor effort.
8. **A small learner-pilot plan:** learners from different disciplines, including learners with no prior AI or business knowledge; observable tasks; success criteria; data to collect; and what findings would trigger revision. AI review and technical checks cannot establish improved learning.

Be demanding and fair. Prefer changes that improve understanding, independent performance, and ease of teaching. If a recommendation conflicts with the no-programming scope, single path, ownership, or adaptable capstone, explain the tradeoff explicitly rather than silently redesigning the course for another audience.
